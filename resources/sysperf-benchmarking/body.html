Section 1

Learning goal & roadmap

Learning goal — what you should be able to do when you close this

Handed a benchmark result — yours, a colleague's, or a vendor's — you can:

  1. Say what was actually measured, as distinct from what the tool's documentation claims it measures.
  2. Name the limiting factor, or say that nobody established one — which is the same as saying the system was never driven to its limit.
  3. Choose the right kind of benchmark for the question being asked, and state what that kind cannot tell you.
  4. Ask the questions that expose the rest: the configuration, the duration, the error rate, the variance, the distribution, and the price.

The core model in one paragraph

A benchmark result is not a fact about your system. It is a fact about how fast the system ran that benchmark, and everything that makes it useful — or worthless — is in the gap between those two statements. The gap has three sources. First, the test may not measure what its name says: tools that claim to test disks routinely test the file system cache instead, and the difference is orders of magnitude. Second, the run may not have reached the thing you meant to measure: it may have been limited by the benchmark software, by an unrelated congested component, by a configuration you did not check, or by errors that returned instantly and were counted as successes. Third, the result may be true and still misleading, because the price, the configuration, or the fact that the product was engineered specifically to score well on this test were left out. The defence against all three is the same and is simple to state: analyse the system while the benchmark is still running. Everything else in these pages is either a catalogue of what goes wrong when you don't, or a technique for doing it well.

Tenth-grader version — the whole thing at once

  • A benchmark measures how fast your computer does the thing the benchmark does. That may not be the thing you care about.
  • The classic mistake: you think you measured A, you actually measured B, and you tell everyone you measured C.
  • Numbers look finished. They have units and decimal points. Nothing about them warns you that the test never touched the disk.
  • So the fix isn't a better tool — it's watching the machine while the test runs, and asking what stopped it going faster.
  • And when someone hands you a number, the useful question isn't “is that good?” It's “what was it, exactly, that you measured?”

What the number actually is

What a benchmark is for, what makes one good, and the four things you must understand before quoting a result.

Tenth-grader version

  • People run benchmarks for about six different reasons, and the reason changes what a good answer looks like.
  • A good benchmark can be repeated, watched while it runs, moved to another machine, explained to your boss, and finished quickly — and it has to resemble something real.
  • Before you quote a number you need four things: what was tested, what stopped it going faster, what else might have interfered, and what you're actually allowed to conclude.
  • The most common way to get all four wrong is to run the test, walk away, and ask an expert to explain the numbers afterwards.
  • By then the machine is idle and nobody can check anything.

The everyday version

Testing a car's fuel economy. The number on the sticker is real — somebody genuinely measured it — but it was measured on a rolling road, at a fixed temperature, with the air conditioning off and a professional driver holding a smooth throttle. None of that is a lie. It is also not your commute. The useful questions are the same four: what exactly was tested (a standardised cycle, not traffic), what limited it (the cycle's own speed profile, not the engine), what might have perturbed it (temperature, tyre pressure, fuel batch), and what may be concluded (you can compare two cars tested the same way; you cannot predict your Tuesday). A benchmark result is a sticker figure, and every argument in this booklet is about reading the small print.

The terms, each with its look-alike

TermPlain definitionConcrete exampleBoundary — what it gets confused with
Benchmarking Testing performance in a controlled manner, so choices can be compared, regressions found, and limits understood — before they are met in production. Those limits may be system resources, software limits imposed by a virtualized environment, or limits in the target application itself. vs a fact about your system. Benchmarks tell you only how fast the system can run the benchmark; it is up to you to understand the result and work out how it applies to your environment. That sentence is the whole subject in one line.
The reasons Six or seven distinct purposes, and the purpose decides what a good result even looks like. System design — comparing systems or components, often for a purchase decision. Proofs of concept — testing under load before committing. Tuning — finding parameters worth investigating with the real workload. Development — non-regression testing, and driving a product to its limit to find where engineering effort pays. Capacity planning — finding limits directly, or data for modelling. Troubleshooting — verifying a component can still reach maximum performance. And marketing — determining maximum product performance for promotional use, sometimes called benchmarketing. It is on the list because it is real, not because it is illegitimate; but a result produced for it answers a different question from one produced for capacity planning.
The six qualities What makes a benchmark good: repeatable (so comparisons are possible), observable (so performance can be analysed and understood), portable (so it runs on competitors and across releases), easily presented (so everyone understands the results), realistic (so measurements reflect what customers experience), runnable (so developers can test changes quickly). When comparing systems with intent to purchase, one more is needed: the price/performance ratio, where price can be quantified as the five-year capital cost of the equipment. Note that observable is doing more work than it appears. A benchmark you cannot watch while it runs cannot be analysed, and an unanalysable benchmark fails the only test that matters in Part 4.
Benchmark analysis The four things you must understand about any result: what is being tested; what the limiting factor or factors are; any perturbations that might affect the results; and what conclusions may be drawn. These require a deep understanding of what the benchmark software does, how the system responds, and how the result relates to the destination environment. vs collecting the number. Given the tool and access to the system, these needs are best served by performance analysis of the system while the benchmark is running — including drill-down analysis to explain and quantify the limiting factors.
The staffing mistake Having junior staff execute the benchmarks and then bringing in performance experts to explain the results after the run has completed. Described as a common mistake, and it is a structural one rather than a personal failing. The correction is scheduling, not skill: engage the experts during the benchmark, so they can analyse the system while it is still running. Once the run has finished, the evidence needed to answer three of the four questions no longer exists.
Custom benchmark software Writing your own tool, or at least your own load generator, instead of adopting one. Kept short and focused on only what your test needs. vs a general tool, which must serve many purposes and is correspondingly larger. The advantage is that a short program is quick to analyse and debug — which matters because you will be analysing it.
Reading someone else's result The case where you have access to neither the tool nor the system. Apply the same four questions to whatever material is available. Plus two that only arise here: what is the system environment, and how is it configured? Without those, the result is a number attached to an unknown machine.

What good analysis actually reads like

A description of an early network stack, written in 1981, is worth reading as a model of the standard being asked for here. Four megabytes were transferred between user processes on two machines, partitioned into 1024-byte records and encapsulated in 1068-byte packets. The transfer took 28 seconds including connection setup and teardown, giving a user-to-user throughput of 1.2 Megabaud. During the transfer the sending machine was CPU saturated while the receiving machine had about 30% idle time. The system time was spread across Ethernet handling (20%), IP packet processing (10%), TCP processing (30%), checksumming (25%) and user system-call handling (15%), with no single part dominating.

Notice what that paragraph does and does not do. It states the workload precisely enough to reproduce. It identifies the limiting factor — one machine was CPU saturated, so the number is a statement about that machine's processor, not about the network. It then decomposes where the time went, which is what turns a number into an explanation. And it stops: it draws no conclusion the measurement does not support. Being able to break down where kernel CPU time goes at this level only became straightforward much later, with flame graphs; this analysis predates them by decades.

Concept boundary — a benchmark is not an experiment about your system

What it is: a controlled measurement of how fast this system ran this particular program under these particular conditions.
What it is not: a prediction of your production performance. Getting from one to the other requires you to establish that the workload resembles yours, that the configuration resembles yours, and that the factor that limited the run is one that would also limit you.
The practical consequence: the sentence “this system does X operations per second” is never a complete claim. The complete claim names the workload, the configuration and the limiter — and a result missing all three is not a weak result, it is an uninterpretable one.

Part 1 · Question 1

A team is choosing between two managed database offerings. An engineer runs a load tool against both for ten minutes each, produces a slide showing one is 1.8× faster, and circulates it. Asked what limited each run, they say the tool reported no errors and both machines “looked busy”. The slide is scheduled for a purchasing decision on Friday. The team's performance specialist is offered a slot on Thursday to “review the numbers”.

(a) Name the four things that must be understood about any benchmark result, and say which of them this exercise has established. (b) Explain why the Thursday review slot is the wrong intervention, in terms of what evidence still exists by then. (c) Name the additional quantity that this particular purpose requires, say how it is defined, and give two further questions you would put to the engineer before Friday.

Part 1 · Question 2

Two results land on your desk on the same morning. The first says: “Storage cluster: 1.4 million operations per second.” The second describes a transfer of a fixed payload between two hosts, states the record and packet sizes, reports the elapsed time and derived throughput, notes that the sending host was CPU saturated while the receiver had roughly a third of its capacity idle, and breaks the system time down across five kernel components, none dominant.

(a) Say what the second result does that the first does not, mapping each to one of the four questions. (b) Explain why the observation about the sending host changes what the throughput figure is a claim about. (c) The first result's author says the second is “overkill for a headline number”. Give the strongest version of their case, then say what you would still require before quoting the 1.4 million figure anywhere.

Carry-out rule from this part

Never quote a benchmark number without its limiter. “X operations per second” is an incomplete claim; “X operations per second, limited by this, on this configuration, running this workload” is a complete one. If nobody can supply the limiter, the run was not driven to its limit — or nobody checked, which for your purposes is the same thing.

The sixteen ways it lies

A catalogue of mistakes, fallacies and misdeeds — most of them ordinary, unglamorous and entirely accidental.

Tenth-grader version

  • The headline failure: you benchmark A, you actually measure B, and you tell everyone you measured C.
  • A popular tool is not a correct tool. “Everyone uses it” is not evidence — it is a named logical fallacy.
  • A number on its own, with no account of what limited it, is usually the start of an investigation, not the end of one.
  • Tests can fail invisibly: every request can error, and the tool will still print a tidy latency figure.
  • Some failures aren't errors at all. The number is true, and the story told about it leaves out the price, or the configuration, or the fact that the product was built to win this exact test.
  • Deliberately faking numbers is the rarest item on the list. Nearly everything here is done by honest people in a hurry.

The everyday version

A restaurant's food-hygiene rating. Most of the ways it can mislead you have nothing to do with dishonesty. The inspection happened on one Tuesday in March, when the head chef was in and the kitchen was quiet — that is ignoring variance. It graded the kitchen, not the food, so you have inferred something the test never measured — that is casual benchmarking. You trust it because everyone does, and you have never read what the grades mean — that is blind faith. Meanwhile the restaurant next door hired a consultant who knows exactly which items the inspector scores and has optimised for those specifically — that is a benchmark special, and it is legal, and the resulting five is genuine. Only one item on the whole list is forging the certificate, and that is the one that almost never happens.

The sixteen, each with its look-alike

FailurePlain definitionConcrete exampleBoundary — what it gets confused with
1 · Casual benchmarking Treating benchmarking as fire-and-forget. Summarised as: you benchmark A, but actually measure B, and conclude you've measured C. Many tools claim or imply they measure disk performance but actually test file system performance. The difference can be orders of magnitude, as file systems use caching and buffering to substitute disk I/O with memory I/O. vs a broken tool. The tool may be functioning correctly and testing the file system exactly as designed — and your conclusions about the disks will still be wildly incorrect. The defect is in the inference, not the software.
2 · Blind faith Believing a benchmark because it is popular, open source, and long-established. The misconception that popularity equals validity is argumentum ad populum. A flawed micro-benchmark promoted by a top technology company, known among veteran performance engineers to be flawed and never to be used — with no easy way to stop it spreading. vs a software bug. The problem is not even necessarily with the benchmark software — though bugs do happen — but with the interpretation of its results. Auditing the code is not sufficient.
3 · Numbers without analysis Bare results with no analytical detail — this can be a sign that the author is inexperienced and has assumed the number is trustworthy and final. Every benchmark number should be accompanied by a description of the limit encountered and the analysis performed. Summarised as: if you've spent less than a week studying a benchmark result, it's probably wrong. vs having no time. If you genuinely don't, the honest move is to list the assumptions you haven't checked and publish them with the result — that the tool isn't buggy, that the disk test measures disk I/O, that the tool drove it to its limit, that this workload is relevant. The list becomes the to-do for verification if the result later matters.
4 · Complex benchmark tools A tool whose own complexity hinders the analysis. Ideally the program is open source so it can be studied, and short enough to be read and understood quickly. Prefer C for micro-benchmarks; for client simulation, use the same language as the client to minimise differences. Complex suites make analysis hard through sheer volume of code. vs the specific failure of benchmarking the benchmark — where the reported result is limited by the benchmark software itself. A common cause is single-threaded benchmark software, which measures one thread's ceiling and reports it as the system's.
5 · Testing the wrong thing
6 · Ignoring the environment		 Running an available benchmark rather than a relevant one; and testing on a system that does not resemble production. Testing disk performance because disk tools exist, when the target workload runs entirely out of file system cache. Or evaluating a database on a default server — default tunables, default file system — when the production servers are tuned for high disk IOPS. vs a benchmark that used to be right. One may have tested an appropriate workload once and not been updated for years. The related organisational version: a team standardises on a benchmark and spends all its performance effort on it — if it doesn't resemble customer workloads, the engineering optimises the wrong behaviour.
7 · Ignoring errors A result is produced; that does not mean the test succeeded. Some — or all — requests may have errored. A web server benchmark reported average latency of over one second. The server had done nothing at all: all requests were blocked by a firewall, and the latency shown was the time for the benchmark client to time out and error. Covered by the earlier failures, but singled out because it is so common. Note the shape: the failure produced a plausible number in the right units, not an obvious crash.
8 · Ignoring variance Applying a steady, consistent workload based on the average of real-world measurements, when the real rate and the real mix vary. A disk workload averaging 500 reads/s and 50 writes/s is simulated at those rates, or at the 10:1 ratio so higher rates can be tested. But writes may arrive in bursts every 10 seconds (asynchronous write-back flushing) while synchronous reads are steady. vs perturbation. This is variance in the workload you are simulating. Bursts can cause real production issues — by queueing the reads — that a steady average simply never reproduces. One way to simulate it is a Markov model in the benchmark.
9 · Ignoring perturbations External activity affecting the result: a timed system backup, monitoring agents collecting once per minute, or — in the cloud — unseen tenants on the same host. The strategy for ironing them out is longer runs — minutes instead of seconds. As a rule, a benchmark should not be shorter than one second. Run it several times and examine the standard deviation; it should be as small as possible. Short tests are unusually perturbed by device interrupts pinning the thread during service routines, kernel scheduling decisions that wait before migrating queued threads to preserve CPU affinity, and CPU cache warmth effects. Also collect the distribution of operation latency — not just total runtime — so outliers can be seen and recorded.
10 · Changing multiple factors Comparing two results without understanding everything that differs between them. Two hosts benchmarked over the network: is the network identical? Is one more hops away, over a slower or more congested link? In the cloud, instances created for a test may land on faster or slower systems, or ones with more tenant contention. The countermeasure is not one careful run but several: test multiple instances and take the median — better, record the distribution — to avoid an outlier caused by testing one unusually fast or slow system.
11 · Benchmark paradox “If your product's chances of winning a benchmark are 50/50, you'll usually lose.” Customers buying on performance often want to be sure, so they run several benchmarks and want the product to win them all. At 50% each, the probability of winning three is 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.125 = 12.5%. vs a reason to ignore benchmarks — ignoring benchmarks is itself a benchmarking pitfall. The paradox is an argument for making your results reliably good, not for opting out. And the odds in practice are worse than the coin-flip they start from.
12 · Benchmarking the competition Producing results showing your product beats a competitor's. Far more difficult than it sounds. Customers don't use a product for five minutes; they use it for months, analysing and tuning it. You do not have weeks to tune a competitor, so you can only gather untuned — and therefore unrealistic — results. The self-defeating part: the competitor's customers, the target of the exercise, may well recognise untuned results, so your company loses credibility with the very people it was trying to impress. If you must do it, tune their product seriously — best practices, customer forums, bug databases, outside expertise — then make the same effort on your own before going head to head.
13 · Friendly fire Underselling your own product: not testing the top-performing configuration, or not driving the system to its true limit. An engineering team's high performance depended on a new technology that had not yet been documented. The benchmark team didn't understand it, misconfigured it, and published numbers that undersold the product. Also counts: benchmarking an older software version with issues already fixed, or on whatever limited equipment happened to be available. Potential customers assume a published company benchmark shows the best possible performance — so not providing that undersells the product. The guard is the question “what is the bottleneck for this benchmark?”, and sharing results with engineering before publication.
14 · Misleading benchmarks Results that are technically correct but misrepresented — through unintentionally limited information about what was measured, or deliberately omitted information. A vendor builds a prohibitively expensive custom product, achieves a fantastic result, does not disclose the price, focuses on non-price/performance metrics, and markets an ambiguous summary (“we are 2× faster!”) associated with the company or product line in general. vs cheating. The numbers are not fake — it is lying by omission. A second variant: a marketing budget engages several third parties, not for expertise but for a fast cheap result, and picks the best of the group; non-expertise is arguably advantageous, since a large deviation in a favourable direction is what is wanted. Treat such results as upper bounds you should not expect to exceed.
15 · Benchmark specials The vendor studies a popular or industry benchmark and engineers the product to score well on it, disregarding actual customer performance. Also called optimising for the benchmark. The term dates from 1993 and the TPC-A benchmark: a database vendor was charged with adding a special option purely to inflate its results — something a typical customer wouldn't use — and replied, with some justification, that it had followed the letter of the specification, which said nothing about benchmark specials. The fix was specification, not argument. An anti-benchmark-special clause now prohibits implementations that improve benchmark results but not real-world performance or pricing, and — since the focus is price/performance — a price requirement that the total price be within 2% of what a customer would pay. These were addressed many years ago and you shouldn't necessarily expect them today.
16 · Cheating Sharing fake results. Described as either rare or nonexistent: no case of purely made-up numbers being shared, even in the most bloodthirsty of benchmarking battles. vs everything above it. This is the failure people imagine when they distrust benchmarks, and it is the least likely one they will meet. Suspecting fraud is almost always a way of not doing the analysis that would find the real cause.

Concept boundary — a wrong measurement is not the same as a wrong claim

A wrong measurement means the number does not describe what you think it describes: the disk test hit the cache, every request errored, the tool's single thread was the ceiling, a backup ran through the middle of it. Fix these by analysing the run.
A wrong claim means the number is accurate and the story around it is not: the price is missing, the configuration is not one anybody would buy, the product was built specifically to win this test, or a specific result has been generalised into a slogan. Fix these by asking for what was left out.
Why the distinction earns its place: the two have different remedies and different tells. No amount of careful measurement protects you from a claim error, and no amount of scepticism about marketing protects you from a measurement error in your own lab. Most people are alert to one kind and blind to the other — and it is usually their own work where they are blind.

Choosing the kind

Micro-benchmark, simulation, replay, industry standard — what each one can answer, and what it structurally cannot.

Tenth-grader version

  • There is a spectrum of benchmark types, arranged by how much they resemble the real workload — with the real production workload sitting at one end of it.
  • A micro-benchmark tests one narrow operation. Easy to understand, easy to root-cause, and obviously artificial — which is a feature, because nobody mistakes it for reality.
  • A simulation imitates a real customer workload from measurements of it. Closer to reality, harder to analyse.
  • A replay plays a recorded trace back at the system. Sounds ideal. Usually isn't.
  • An industry-standard benchmark is one an independent body defines, documents and audits, so results across vendors can be compared at all.
  • Designing micro-benchmarks means multiplying: the tests you want, times how much data, times how many threads. And after you have tested the good weather, test the bad.

The everyday version

Ways to find out whether someone can drive. A micro-benchmark is the reverse-parking exercise on an empty test pad: one skill, isolated, repeatable, and obviously not a journey — you would never mistake the score for “can drive”. A simulation is the driving test, built from a survey of what real driving contains: 40% urban, 19% junctions, 7% motorway, and the mix is chosen deliberately. A replay is putting the candidate in a simulator that plays back the exact route somebody else drove last Tuesday — the trouble is that when the candidate behaves differently, the recording carries on regardless, because the traffic in it was never going to react to them. And the industry standard is the national licence: defined by an independent body, run to published rules, and comparable between one candidate and another precisely because nobody gets to design their own exam.

Stateless vs stateful simulation Stateless: each server request is unrelated to the previous one — a series of operations, each type chosen randomly according to the measured probability. Reads and writes may arrive in groups, so the probability of a write following a write is much higher than a write following a read. Such a workload is better simulated with a Markov model, representing requests as states and measuring the probability of state transitions. This is the same instrument Part 2 named as the answer to ignoring variance — and it is needed here for the same reason.
Ageing of simulations Customer usage patterns change over time, so simulations need updating and adjusting to stay relevant. Two well-known CPU benchmarks are simulations of their era: one developed in 1972 to simulate the scientific workloads of the time, another from 1984 simulating the integer-based workloads of the time. There may be resistance to updating, because published results based on the older version would no longer be comparable with the new one. Replay Capturing a trace log and replaying it at the target, testing performance with the actual captured client operations. Sounds ideal — as good as testing in production. It is problematic: when characteristics and delivered latency change on the server, the captured client workload is unlikely to respond naturally to those differences, which may prove no better than a simulated workload. When too much faith is placed in it, things get worse. The worked case: a storage upgrade evaluated by replaying the production trace on new hardware. Performance was worse and the sale was lost. The real workload would have improved, returning largely from cache, which replaying disk events never simulated.

Vendors may participate, usually for a fee, which provides the software to execute the benchmark. A result usually requires full disclosure of the configured environment, which may be audited. vs running your own. For the customer these save a lot of time, since results may already exist for many vendors and products. Your task becomes finding the benchmark that most closely resembles your future or current production workload — and for a current one, that is decided by workload characterization.
The two bodies, and the common metrics The Transaction Processing Performance Council (TPC) creates and administers industry benchmarks focused on database performance. TPC-C, a complete computing environment where a population of users executes transactions; TPC-DS, a decision support system with queries and data maintenance; TPC-E, an OLTP workload modelling a brokerage firm's database; TPC-H, decision support with ad hoc queries and concurrent modifications; TPC-VMS, allowing other benchmarks to be gathered for virtualized databases; TPCx-HS, a big data benchmark; TPCx-V, database workloads in virtual machines. TPC results include price/performance. SPEC results include how systems were tuned and a component list, but not usually their price.
Cross-vendor metrics Single figures intended to be comparable across products, in the way miles per gallon is for cars. The need was set out in a 1985 paper, “A Measure of Transaction Processing Power”, which described the need to measure the price/performance ratio, detailed three benchmarks vendors could execute — Sort, Scan and DebitCredit — and proposed an industry-standard transactions per second (TPS) measure based on DebitCredit. That work later encouraged the creation of TPC. Others used for the same role: MIPS, millions of instructions per second — a measure of performance, but the work performed depends on the type of instruction, which can be difficult to compare between processor architectures; and FLOPS, floating-point operations per second, the same role for floating-point-heavy workloads. Industry benchmarks typically measure a custom metric that serves only for comparison with itself.

Concept boundary — realism and analysability trade against each other

The spectrum: benchmark types line up by how much they resemble the real workload, with the production workload itself at one end. Micro-benchmarks sit at the artificial end; simulations in the middle; replay closest to production.
What moving toward realism buys: results that resemble what clients will actually experience, including complex system interactions a narrow test would miss entirely.
What it costs: analysability. A micro-benchmark involves few components and few code paths, so a difference can be root-caused quickly and the test is repeatable because other variation is factored out. A realistic workload is realistic precisely because it does not factor anything out.
The trap at the far end: replay looks like it escapes the trade-off — real operations, real system — but a recording cannot react. Change the server's delivered latency and the captured client keeps issuing what it issued before. It buys realism of input while losing realism of behaviour, which is why it can be worse than an honest simulation.

Analyse it while it runs

One habit prevents or exposes most of Part 2 — and the methods, checklists and questions that hang off it.

Tenth-grader version

  • The bad way: start the test, walk away, put the numbers on a slide, hand the slide to management. That is a named anti-method, not a straw man.
  • The good way: watch the machine while the test is running, with ordinary observability tools, and check that it is doing what the tool's name claims.
  • Doing this once — on a well-known drive benchmark — showed that the test never touched a disk. It was writing one byte at a time into memory.
  • Keep asking “why not double?” If you can't say what stopped it going twice as fast, you have not found the limit yet.
  • Do the arithmetic. If the claimed result would need more bandwidth than the cable has, the result is wrong and no further debate is required.
  • And when you push load higher, watch the latency. A bigger number at an unacceptable latency is not a bigger number.

The everyday version

Two ways to check a claim that a lorry can carry ten tonnes. The first: send it away loaded, wait for it to come back, and read the ticket it brings you. The second: stand on the weighbridge while it drives on. The second way is not more rigorous in principle — it is just the only one where you can see that the trailer was half empty, that the load was polystyrene, that the driver went round the block instead of up the hill, and that the weighbridge itself tops out at eight tonnes. All of that is visible for exactly as long as the lorry is on the bridge, and none of it is recoverable from the ticket. Active benchmarking is standing on the weighbridge; passive benchmarking is reading the ticket.

The analysis case study A well-known micro-benchmark suite whose documentation describes it as a program to test hard drive performance. Its first test scored 739 Kbytes/sec in a per-character sequential-output column. Sanity check first: if that really were I/O per character, the system would be achieving 739,000 I/O per second — implausible for disks. Disk statistics during the run reported no disk I/O. Block-layer tracing showed no I/O issued and none completed, but buffers dirtied: 808,225 dirty-buffer and 1,025,678 touch-buffer events. Cache statistics showed a pure workload of dirties — hundreds of thousands per second, no misses. Drilling further up the stack: at the virtual file system layer the process had made 1,176,936 write calls against 13 reads and 7 opens, and the size histogram put 668,839 of them at 1 byte. Conclusion: the first test is a 1-byte file system write that buffers in the file system cache, and does not test disk I/O as the description implies. Its no-write-buffering option turned out not to synchronise after every write, and its direct-I/O option failed — so there was no way to do a per-character disk write test at all.
What the case study is not Not an argument that the tool is unusually bad — it has served people well on many occasions, and was chosen because it is well known and previously studied, with its most suspicious test deliberately picked. Some would argue it isn't broken: “sequential output” and “per character” never promised disk I/O. For a benchmark that claims to test hard drive performance, that is at least misleading. A second lesson hides in its history. Older versions let the C library buffer writes before they reached the file system, so the observed write size was 4 Kbytes or more depending on the library and OS version — making cross-OS comparison meaningless. Fixing it created a new problem: new results cannot be compared with old ones.

Resource sweep during the run Applying the utilization / saturation / errors check to every resource while the benchmark runs. Its purpose here is narrow and decisive: to ensure that a limit is found. The disjunction is the useful part. Either some component, hardware or software, has reached 100% utilization — or you are not driving the system to its limit. There is no third outcome, and “the numbers looked busy” is not one of the two.
Workload characterization
during benchmarking		 Characterizing the production workload for comparison, to determine how well a given benchmark relates to your current production environment. It is the method that answers “which industry-standard benchmark resembles us?” and “is this simulation still relevant?” vs analysing the benchmark. This one analyses production, and uses the result to judge the benchmark. It is the only method here that does not require the benchmark to be running.
Custom benchmarks Coding the software yourself for simple benchmarks. Keep the program as short as possible, to avoid complexity that hinders analysis. C is usually a good choice for micro-benchmarks as it maps closely to what is executed — but think about compiler optimisations: the compiler may elide simple benchmark routines if it thinks the output is unused. It may be worth disassembling the binary to see what will actually run. vs any language with a virtual machine, asynchronous garbage collection or dynamic runtime compilation, which are much harder to debug and control with reliable precision — though you may need them anyway to simulate client software written in them. Always check with other tools while it runs to confirm its operation. A side benefit: writing one can reveal API options for improving performance that production was built too early to use.
Ramping load Determining maximum throughput by adding load in small increments and measuring delivered throughput until a limit is reached. Graph the results to get a scalability profile, studied visually or with scalability models. A file system and server scaled with threads, each performing 8 Kbyte random reads on a cached file, added one by one. The system peaked at almost half a million reads per second, checked at the virtual file system layer, which confirmed the 8 Kbyte size and over 3.5 Gbytes/s transferred at peak. The load generator was short enough to print in full, and read directly through the system call to avoid buffering. The limit turned out to be CPU on the server, which prompted a further investigation. vs pushing until the number stops rising. Measure latency as well as throughput, especially the distribution. Near the limit, queueing delays make latency climb; push too far and the result is no longer reasonable to consider valid. The worked judgement: 990,000 IOPS at 5 ms average, or scraping past 1 million at over 50 ms average with everything heavily queued. The answer you give marketing is 990,000. Ask whether the delivered latency would be acceptable to a customer.
Sanity check Checking a result by investigating whether any characteristic doesn't make sense — in particular, whether it would have required a component to exceed a known limit: network bandwidth, controller bandwidth, interconnect bandwidth, or disk IOPS. A file server benchmarked with 8 Kbyte reads reported 50,000 IOPS on a single 1 Gbit/s interface. 50,000 × 8 Kbytes = 400,000 Kbytes/s, plus protocol headers — over 3.2 Gbit/s, well in excess of the known limit. Results like this usually mean the benchmark tested client caching and never drove the whole workload to the server. Real single-direction results collected over one 1 Gbit/s interface: 120 Mbyte/s (0.96 Gbit/s) may be fine, as such an interface should reach around 119 Mbyte/s in practice; 200 Mbyte/s (1.6 Gbit/s) is possible only if traffic in both directions was summed, which these were not; and 350, 800 and 1,150 Mbyte/s (2.8, 6.4 and 9.2 Gbit/s) are clearly bogus. In most cases this exercise ultimately discovers the result is bogus. With access to the system you can go further and follow the scientific method: the question under test becomes whether the benchmark result is valid.
Statistical analysis Three phases: 1. selection of the tool, its configuration, and the system metrics to capture; 2. execution, collecting a large dataset of results and metrics; 3. interpretation with statistical analysis, producing a report. Used where access to a large-scale system is time-limited and expensive — one “max config” system, and many teams wanting it: sales for proofs of concept, marketing for the best numbers, support for pathologies that appear only at that scale, engineering for new features, quality for non-regression testing and certification. Each has limited time on the machine and much more time afterwards. vs active benchmarking, which analyses the system while the benchmark runs; and vs passive benchmarking, in which no analysis is performed at all. Because collection is expensive, make an extra effort to ensure metrics are reliable, and collect more statistical properties — variation, full distributions, error margins — so problems surface sooner. For code changes and non-regression testing, understanding variation and error margins is crucial to making sense of a pair of results. Collect as much performance data as the overhead allows, so forensic analysis is possible afterwards.
The benchmarking checklist Six questions to seek answers to from any benchmark, to verify its accuracy. Why not double? — why was the rate not double the result? This is really asking what the limiter is, and answering it solves many benchmarking problems, when you discover the limiter is not the intended target of the test. Did it break limits? — the sanity check. Did it error? — errors perform differently from normal operations, and a high error rate skews results. Does it reproduce? — how consistent are the results? Does it matter? — the workload tested may not be relevant to your production needs; some micro-benchmarks test individual system and library calls your application may not even use. Did it even happen? — the firewall case from Part 2, where a blocked benchmark reported timeout-based latency as its result.
Questions for a result you cannot analyse A longer list, for when a vendor gives you a result and you have no access to the running benchmark. The goal: determine what is really being measured, and how realistic or repeatable it is. In general: does it relate to my production workload; what was the configuration of the system under test; a single system or a cluster; what is its cost; what was the client configuration; what was the duration and how many results; is it an average or a peak, and what is the average; what are the distribution details; what was the limiting factor; what was the success/fail ratio; what were the operation attributes and how were they chosen; does it simulate variance or an average workload; was it confirmed with other analysis tools — provide screenshots; can an error margin be expressed; is it reproducible. Then the resource-specific lists. CPU and memory: which processors, overclocked, custom cooling, how many memory modules and how attached, any CPUs disabled, system-wide CPU utilization (lightly loaded systems can perform faster due to higher turbo boosting), cores or hyperthreads, how much and what type of main memory, custom firmware settings. Storage: device configuration including count, type, protocol, RAID, cache size and write-back or write-through; file system configuration and tuning; the working set size; to what degree and where it cached; how many files. Network: interface count, type and configuration; topology; protocols and socket options; stack tunables. For industry benchmarks, many of these are answered by the disclosure.

Concept boundary — three things people call “analysing the benchmark”

Passive: no analysis at all. The objective is data collection; the analysis step does not exist.
Active: analysis of the system, while the benchmark runs. This is the only one that can establish what was actually measured, because that evidence exists only while the load is applied.
Statistical: analysis of the results, after the run. Legitimate and necessary — it is how you handle expensive, contended, time-limited hardware — but it answers different questions: consistency, variation, error margins, distributions.
Why the distinction matters: statistical analysis can tell you a result is reproducible. It cannot tell you the result was of the right thing. A benchmark that measures the file system cache instead of the disk will reproduce beautifully, with a tiny standard deviation, forever.

Glossary

TermMeaning
BenchmarkingTesting performance in a controlled manner, so choices can be compared and limits understood before they are met in production. A benchmark tells you only how fast the system ran the benchmark.
BenchmarketingBenchmarking to determine maximum product performance for promotional use. A legitimate purpose that answers a different question from capacity planning.
Price/performance ratioPerformance per unit of cost; the extra quality required when comparing systems with intent to purchase. Price may be quantified as the five-year capital cost of the equipment.
Casual benchmarkingFire-and-forget benchmarking: you benchmark A, actually measure B, and conclude you have measured C.
Argumentum ad populum“Appeal to the people” — the misconception that popularity equals validity. The named fallacy behind blind faith in a well-known benchmark.
Benchmarking the benchmarkWhen the reported result is limited by the benchmark software itself. A common cause is single-threaded benchmark software.
PerturbationExternal activity affecting a result: a scheduled backup, a monitoring agent, an unseen tenant on the same host, or short-run effects such as interrupts, scheduling decisions and cache warmth.
Markov modelA model representing requests as states and measuring the probability of transitions between them; the instrument for simulating variance and stateful workloads rather than a flat average mix.
Benchmark paradoxIf your product's chances of winning a benchmark are 50/50, you will usually lose — because customers run several and want you to win them all.
Friendly fireUnderselling your own product by benchmarking a suboptimal configuration, an old version, limited equipment, or never driving the system to its true limit.
Lying by omissionA benchmark result that is technically correct but misrepresented by leaving out the price, the configuration, or the scope of the claim. Not the same as fake numbers.
Benchmark specialEngineering the product to score well on a popular or industry benchmark while disregarding actual customer performance. Also called optimising for the benchmark.
Micro-benchmarkAn artificial workload testing one particular type of operation. Simple, repeatable, quick to root-cause, and deliberately not mistakable for a real workload.
Simulation (macro-benchmark)A benchmark imitating a customer application workload, usually built from workload characterization of production.
Stateless / stateful simulationStateless: each request is independent, its type drawn randomly from measured probabilities. Stateful: each request depends on client state, at minimum the previous request.
ReplayReplaying a captured trace log against the target. Problematic because a recording cannot respond naturally to a server whose characteristics and delivered latency have changed.
Working set sizeThe size of the data being accessed. Much smaller than main memory tests the file system software; much larger minimises cache and drives the test toward disk I/O.
Sunny day testingBenchmarks focused on top speeds. Its complement is cloudy or rainy day testing: non-ideal situations including contention, perturbations and workload variance.
Industry-standard benchmarkA benchmark from an independent organisation: well defined, documented, executed under guidelines, and usually requiring full disclosure of the configured environment, which may be audited.
TPS / MIPS / FLOPSCross-vendor measures — transactions, millions of instructions, and floating-point operations per second. Instruction-based measures are hard to compare across processor architectures; industry suites usually report a custom metric that compares only with itself.
Passive benchmarkingThe anti-methodology: run the benchmark, ignore it until it finishes, collect the data. No analysis is performed.
Active benchmarkingAnalysing performance while the benchmark runs, using observability tools — to confirm what is really being tested and to find the true limiter of the system or of the benchmark.

Answer key

Part 1 · Q1 — the ten-minute comparison and the Thursday review

(a) The four are: what is being tested; what the limiting factor or factors are; any perturbations that might affect the results; and what conclusions may be drawn. This exercise has established none of them. The tool's name is standing in for the first, “looked busy” is not a limiting factor — the resource sweep offers only two outcomes, something at 100% or a system not driven to its limit, and neither has been shown — nothing was checked for perturbations, and the conclusion (1.8×) has been drawn without any of the three that would license it. “No errors reported” is worth something but is not one of the four. (b) Because three of the four questions can only be answered from the system while the load is applied, and by Thursday the machines are idle. This is the staffing mistake in its usual form: junior staff execute the benchmarks and performance experts are brought in to explain the results afterwards. The correction is scheduling rather than skill — engage the expert during the run, so they can analyse the live system with drill-down to quantify the limiter. If Thursday is genuinely all there is, the useful output is a list of the assumptions nobody had time to check, published with the result as a to-do list for verification. (c) The purpose here is a purchase, so the required extra quantity is the price/performance ratio, where price can be quantified as the five-year capital cost of the equipment. Further questions worth putting: what was the limiting factor in each run; what was the configuration of each system, and does either resemble what we would actually buy and run; was the workload representative of ours, and how was that established; how long did each run last and how many times was it repeated; what were the success and failure counts; is this an average or a peak, and what is the distribution.

Part 1 · Q2 — the bare figure and the decomposed one

(a) The second result answers the four questions and the first answers none. What is being tested: the second states the payload size, the record size and the packet size, precisely enough to reproduce; the first says only “operations”. What the limiting factor is: the second says the sending host was CPU saturated while the receiver had roughly a third idle; the first offers nothing. Perturbations: the second's decomposition of system time across five components, none dominant, shows that nothing unexpected was consuming the machine; the first gives no basis to check. What may be concluded: the second stops at what the measurement supports and draws no wider claim; the first's single number is already a claim about a cluster. (b) Because a saturated sender makes the throughput a statement about that machine's processor, not about the network or the receiving system. The same wire and the same receiver with a faster sender would give a different figure, so the number cannot be quoted as a property of the link or of the pair. Naming the limiter is what converts a measurement into a claim with a subject. (c) The strongest version of their case: a headline figure exists to be compared with other headline figures, decomposition costs analyst time that a purchasing conversation will not read, and if the cluster genuinely served 1.4 million operations then it did so regardless of where the kernel time went. What you would still require before quoting it: what the operations actually were and at what size; what limited the run, established by observing the system rather than asserted; whether any component was at 100%, since if none was the figure is a floor rather than a ceiling; the error and timeout counts; the duration and the number of repeats with their variation; and the configuration, so the reader knows what machine the number is about. Absent those, the honest form is the number plus an explicit list of the assumptions nobody checked.

Part 2 · Q1 — the two brokers

(a) Four named failures. Complex benchmark tools, in its specific form benchmarking the benchmark: a single-process client means the reported result may be the tool's own ceiling rather than either broker's — measurement wrong. Changing multiple factors: one broker acknowledged writes durably and the other did not, so the two runs differ in more than the product — measurement wrong. Ignoring errors: roughly 8% of B's requests errored and were counted as completed operations, and errors perform differently from normal operations and skew the result — measurement wrong. Ignoring perturbations: an index rebuild ran on the B host through the last six seconds of a twenty-second run — measurement wrong. All four are in the accidental-measurement quadrant, which is where ten of the sixteen live. (b) Two near-free remedies. For perturbations: make the runs longer — minutes instead of seconds — with the rule that a benchmark's duration should not be shorter than one second, then run it several times and examine the standard deviation, which should be as small as possible. For errors: check the operation success/fail ratio before reading any throughput figure; the checklist puts this as “did it error?” and “did it even happen?”. (c) The slide commits numbers without analysis: bare results with no analytical detail, which is a sign the author has assumed they are trustworthy and final. Every benchmark number should be accompanied by a description of the limit encountered and the analysis performed. Failing that, the slide should have carried the list of assumptions nobody had time to check — that the tool is not the bottleneck, that the two configurations are equivalent, that the operations succeeded, that nothing else was running — which then becomes the verification to-do list if the decision turns out to matter.

Part 2 · Q2 — four benchmarks and three proposals

(a) 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.0625, so 6.25% — about one chance in sixteen. The rule is the benchmark paradox: “if your product's chances of winning a benchmark are 50/50, you'll usually lose”, because customers who want to be sure run several and expect you to win them all, and the more benchmarks the worse the chances. (b) Proposal (i) is benchmarking the competition, and in its cheap-third-party form it is also the misleading benchmarks variant where several firms are engaged not for expertise but for a fast inexpensive result and the best is kept. It backfires because customers use a product for months and tune it, while you have days — so you can only produce untuned and therefore unrealistic results, and the competitor's customers, who are the target of the exercise, will recognise them, costing your company credibility with exactly the people it was trying to impress. Proposal (ii) is a benchmark special: engineering the product to score on the benchmark while disregarding actual customer performance. The industry bodies answered it with specification rather than argument — an anti-benchmark-special clause prohibiting all implementations that improve benchmark results but not real-world performance or pricing — and accompanied it with a price requirement that the total price be within 2% of what a customer would actually pay, closing the parallel trick of quoting deep discounts nobody would receive. (c) Proposal (iii) commits the pitfall that ignoring benchmarks is itself a benchmarking failure. The intervention that genuinely improves the odds is to stop being a coin flip and to make sure your own results are the best your product can honestly produce — the friendly-fire remedy: test the top-performing system and configuration, drive it to its true limit and be able to say what the bottleneck was, benchmark the current version on the best available equipment, and share the results with engineering before publication so they can spot what was misconfigured or left off.

Part 3 · Q1 — replaying block-level traffic at a new storage tier

(a) It will show a regression, and the regression will be an artefact. The trace was captured at the block device layer, which means it records the requests the old system's cache failed to absorb. Replaying those against the new tier asks its slower spinning media to service, from disk, precisely the traffic the new tier's four-times-larger cache was bought to absorb — so the new tier is measured on its weakest component with its strongest one bypassed. The real production workload would have improved, returning largely from cache, which replaying device-level events cannot simulate. It is the storage-upgrade case exactly: 10K rpm disks replaced by 7,200 rpm disks with sixteen times the file system cache, a replay that showed a regression, and a lost sale; it is a case of testing the wrong thing. (b) Even at the right level, a replay disappoints because a recording cannot react: when the server's characteristics and delivered latency change, the captured client workload is unlikely to respond naturally to those differences, which may prove no better than a simulated workload — and when too much faith is placed in it, worse. Subtle timing effects can also disturb it. (c) Every micro-benchmark test is multiplied by working set size and thread count. Working set much smaller than main memory caches entirely, so the file system software is what you investigate; much larger than main memory minimises cache and drives the test toward disk I/O. Thread count, assuming a small working set: single-threaded tests file system performance at the current CPU clock speed; enough threads to saturate all CPUs tests the maximum of system, file system and CPUs together. To answer “does the extra memory help?” you need both settings of the working set on both tiers, and in particular a working set sized to fit the new tier's memory but not the old tier's — that is the cell where the extra cache is the only thing that differs. Run single-threaded to attribute the result to the software path and saturating to establish the ceiling.

Part 3 · Q2 — the six-year-old internal benchmark

(a) It is a simulation, also called a macro-benchmark: a benchmark imitating a customer application workload, normally built from workload characterization of the production environment. The weakness this is an instance of is that customer usage patterns change over time, so simulations require updating and adjusting to stay relevant — and there is resistance to that when published results based on the older version would no longer be usable for comparison with the new one. (b) What is being traded is comparability against relevance: two years of quarterly results form a series that only holds together while the benchmark is unchanged, and the benchmark is only worth running while it resembles the traffic. Either way, keep the two years and relabel them as measurements of the old workload — they remain valid for comparison with each other and invalid as statements about current performance. Do not plot old and new on one line; start a new series and say plainly which property you chose. (c) An independent body's suite offers: tests that are well defined and documented and must be executed under guidelines so results are as intended; full disclosure of the configured environment, which may be audited, and depending on the body either price/performance or a component and tuning list; and results that already exist for a variety of vendors and products, so you can compare without running anything. The question you must answer first is whether it resembles your production workload — for a current workload that is decided by workload characterization, measuring the real makeup of your traffic and comparing it against what the suite exercises.

Part 4 · Q1 — 400,000 reads per second over a 10 Gbit/s link

(a) The sanity check: 400,000 operations per second × 4 Kbytes = 1,600,000 Kbytes/s, which is about 1.6 Gbytes/s, or roughly 12.8 Gbit/s — before protocol headers — over a single 10 Gbit/s interface. That exceeds the known limit of the link, so the result is not merely doubtful, it is impossible, and the conclusion is that the figure is bogus. No further debate is needed; the arithmetic settles it. (b) The likely explanation is that the benchmark tested client caching and never drove the entire workload to the server, which is what results of this shape usually mean. During the run this would have been caught by observing the server — counting the operations actually arriving there and comparing them with what the clients reported — which is active benchmarking rather than reading the client's summary. (c) “No component reached saturation” is a finding in its own right, because the resource sweep offers exactly two outcomes: either some hardware or software component has reached 100% utilization, or you are not driving the system to its limit. Since nothing did, the second holds, so the figure is a floor rather than a ceiling and no limiting factor has been established — meaning the result cannot honestly be quoted at all. The checklist puts the same point in three words: “Why not double?”

Part 4 · Q2 — four hours on contended hardware

(a) Statistical analysis, which is the approach used where access to a large-scale system is both time-limited and expensive and many teams — sales, marketing, support, engineering, quality — want the same machine. Its three phases: selection of the benchmark tool, its configuration and the system performance metrics to capture; execution of the benchmark, collecting a large dataset of results and metrics; and interpretation of the data with statistical analysis, producing a report. It can establish consistency, variation, error margins and full distributions. It cannot establish what was actually measured — that evidence exists only while the load is applied, which is active benchmarking's job. (b) Because when benchmarking for code changes or non-regression testing it is crucial to understand the variation and the error margins in order to make sense of a pair of results. A claimed 15% difference means nothing if run-to-run variation is of the same order, and a single before-and-after pair contains no information about that variation at all. (c) During the four hours, beyond running the two configurations: analyse the system while the load is applied — confirm the benchmark is testing what it claims, sweep resources for utilization, saturation and errors until either something is at 100% or you accept the system was not driven to its limit, and profile both target and benchmark software to see whether anything unexpected is limiting it. Also collect as much performance data as the collection overhead allows, including variation, full distributions and error margins, so forensic analysis is possible afterwards; a script that dumps every available counter before, during and after each run is the standard form. It has to happen then because the machine is not yours next month, because the metrics are expensive enough that a problem found later means redoing them, and because what was measured is unrecoverable once the load stops. (d) Three of the six, with what each requires: Why not double? — needs the limiter, established by the resource sweep and profiling during the run. Does it reproduce? — needs repeated runs and their standard deviation, which is also what makes the pair of results interpretable. Did it error? — needs the operation success/fail ratio, since errors perform differently from normal operations and a high error rate skews the result. (Equally acceptable: did it break limits?, needing the arithmetic against known component limits; does it matter?, needing workload characterization of production; did it even happen?, needing confirmation at the target that the work arrived.)
