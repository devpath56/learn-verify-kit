One warning before you start

Almost every number in this competency is a negotiation, not a calculation. An availability target, a canary population, a metric threshold — each is a claim about what users will tolerate, agreed by people who can be wrong. If you finish able to compute an error budget but unable to say who has to agree to it and what they give up, the booklet has failed.

Learning goal & roadmap

Learning goal — what you should be able to do when you close this

Given a real reliability decision (“what should our target be?”, “we blew the budget, now what?”, “is this release safe to push?”, “users are unhappy but we're inside SLO”), you can:

  1. Name the instrument — indicator, objective, budget, policy — and say which one the argument is actually about.
  2. Locate the disagreement between what you measure and what users feel, and say which of the two is wrong.
  3. Prescribe the move — tighten, loosen, change the implementation, make it aspirational, canary it — and name the near-miss you rejected.
  4. State the price. Every one of these costs engineering time, feature velocity, customer experience, or credibility.

The core model in one paragraph

Perfect reliability is unbuyable and, past a point, unwanted: each extra nine costs more while its value to the customer approaches zero, and a system that can never change dies slowly. So you pick a target below 100%, and the gap between it and perfection becomes a budget — a quantity of unreliability you may spend. That converts an argument about opinions into arithmetic: what did this outage cost, is this project worth more than that one, may we ship today. And once unreliability is a currency, the question stops being how to avoid spending it and becomes how to spend it well — which is what canarying is: information about a release, bought at a small bounded price.

The whole thing for a smart 15-year-old

  • Nothing is perfect, and chasing perfect costs more and more for less and less.
  • So decide how much failure is acceptable, and treat the rest as an allowance you're allowed to spend.
  • Now arguments about "is it reliable enough?" become arithmetic, and everybody has to agree the number in advance.
  • Then spend a little of that allowance on purpose — show a new version to a few users first — so you find out it's broken cheaply instead of expensively.

Why 100% is the wrong target

The objective below perfection, the budget that falls out of it, and who has to own the trade.

Tenth-grader version

  • Aiming for "never broken" sounds responsible. It's actually the most expensive possible goal, and you still won't hit it.
  • Your users don't experience your system directly — there's a long chain of other things between you and them, any of which can break.
  • If you promise perfection you can never change anything, and the biggest cause of outages is change.
  • So pick a number below perfect. The gap between that number and perfect is an allowance you're allowed to spend.

Start with the delivery promise

  • A courier can promise “next day, every time, no exceptions.” To keep it, they need spare vans, spare drivers, spare depots — and even then a bridge closure defeats them.
  • Going from 99% to 99.9% on-time means buying a second depot. From 99.9% to 99.99% means a third. Each nine costs more than the last, while the customer's happiness barely moves — nobody notices the difference between one late parcel a year and one every ten years.
  • And a courier who promises perfection can never reroute, never try a faster van, never change anything — because every change risks the promise.
  • So they promise something achievable and keep the difference as room to manoeuvre. That room is the whole idea.

The four reasons perfection is wrong

ReasonWhat it actually says
You can't reach itEven with redundant components, automated health checking and fast failover, there is a nonzero probability that one or more components fail simultaneously — which puts availability under 100%.
Users wouldn't see it anywayEven if your system were perfect, the chain of systems between you and your customers is long and complex, and any of it can fail. So as you go 99% → 99.9% → 99.99%, each extra nine costs more while the marginal utility to your customers steadily approaches zero.
You could never changeTo hold perfection you can never update or improve. And the number one source of outages is change — new features, security patches, new hardware, scaling to meet demand. Sooner or later the service stagnates and customers leave, which is not great for anyone's bottom line.
It makes you purely reactiveAt 100% you can do nothing but react to the sub-100% availability that is guaranteed to happen. Stated sharply: reliability of 100% is not an engineering culture objective — it's an operations team objective.

The terms, each with its look-alike

TermPlain definitionConcrete exampleBoundary — what it gets confused with
Service level objective
SLO		 A target level of reliability for the service's customers. Above it, almost all users should be happy; below it, they start complaining or leave. 97% availability. 90% of requests faster than 450 ms. vs a service level agreement (SLA): an SLA is a business contract that comes into effect when users are so unhappy you have to compensate them. An SLO is an internal target you set before anyone is owed money.
Service level indicator
SLI		 An indicator of the level of service you are providing — the thing you measure. The proportion of HTTP requests that succeeded. vs the objective: the indicator is the measurement, the objective is the target you hold it to. One indicator can carry several objectives at different thresholds.
Error budget 100% minus the SLO — the quantity of unreliability you are permitted to spend in a period. A 99.9% success-ratio objective on a service taking 3 million requests over four weeks gives a budget of 3,000 errors. A single outage causing 1,500 of them costs 50% of the budget. vs uptime or a reporting metric: a budget is spendable. If nobody may act on its exhaustion it isn't a budget, it's a number on a dashboard.
Error budget policy A written statement of what happens, and who does it, when the budget is exhausted. Development gives top priority to reliability bugs from the past four weeks; or focuses exclusively on reliability until back in objective; or a production freeze halts certain changes. vs the objective itself: the objective says what “good” is; the policy is what gives it teeth. Getting the policy approved is also the best test of whether the objective is fit for purpose.
Reliability How the service is performing with regard to all of its indicators. Could mean availability, latency, or several at once. vs availability alone, which is one indicator among many. Freshness, durability, correctness, quality and coverage all count as reliability too.

Who owns the number

Once the objective is below 100%, it must be owned by someone empowered to make tradeoffs between feature velocity and reliability. In a small organisation that may be the CTO; in a larger one it is normally the product owner or product manager. An objective owned by the people who merely operate the service is an objective nobody can trade against, which means it will lose every argument it has with a launch date.

Part 1 continued

The four preconditions — without these, none of it works

To adopt an error-budget-based approach, all four must hold:

  1. There are objectives that all stakeholders have approved as fit for the product.
  2. The people responsible for meeting them agree it is possible to do so under normal circumstances.
  3. The organisation has committed to using the budget for decision making and prioritising, formalised in an error budget policy.
  4. There is a process for refining the objective.
Otherwise, “SLO compliance will simply be another KPI (key performance indicator) or reporting metric, rather than a decision-making tool.”

Three places teams actually start from

Maturity levelWhat it looks like
GreenfieldNothing deployed yet. Nothing to measure, and no habits to unlearn.
Production, no objectivesMonitoring exists and notifies you when things go awry, but there are no formal objectives, no concept of an error budget, and an unspoken goal of 100% uptime.
An objective without teethA running deployment with an objective below 100%, but no common understanding of its importance or how to use it for continuous improvement. The number exists; nothing follows from it.

Why this is a prioritisation tool, not a reporting one

Engineers are scarce even at the largest organisations, so engineering time should go to the most important characteristics of the most important services. The budget makes that comparison arithmetic rather than rhetoric: given two candidate reliability projects — say automating rollbacks versus moving to a replicated data store — you estimate each one's impact on the error budget and fund the one that buys more. Day to day, the work of the people defending reliability is driven by the objectives: defending them in the short term, and keeping them defensible in the medium to long term.

Concept boundary — what an error budget is not

It is not a licence to be careless, and it is not a measure of how much downtime you have “left” before someone is angry.
It is a decision-making instrument: a quantity you can attribute to specific incidents and specific projects, so that arguments about what to fund become comparisons between numbers rather than between opinions. Its authority comes entirely from the fact that everyone agreed to it in advance.

What to measure, and how to get a first number

Good events over total events, specification versus implementation, and rounding reality into an objective.

Tenth-grader version

  • Measure reliability as a fraction: things that went well, divided by all the things.
  • That always gives you a number between 0 and 100, which makes everything downstream easier.
  • There's a difference between what you want to measure and how you actually measure it — and the how is where all the compromises hide.
  • Your first number doesn't have to be right. It has to exist, and it has to be improvable.

Start with the bus route

  • You want to know if the bus route is any good. “Buses that arrived within five minutes of schedule, divided by all scheduled buses” — a fraction, 0 to 100%.
  • Now: how do you count it? The driver's log is free but misses buses that never left the depot. An inspector at one stop is more accurate but covers one stop. A GPS unit on every bus is best and costs the most.
  • Same question — “are buses on time?” — three different instruments, each with different quality (how well it reflects a passenger's experience), coverage (how many passengers it speaks for), and cost.
  • That gap between the question and the instrument is the single most important idea in this part.

The shape of an indicator

  • Treat the indicator as the number of good events divided by the total number of events. Examples:
    • Successful HTTP requests / total HTTP requests (a success rate).
    • gRPC calls that completed successfully in under 100 ms / total gRPC requests.
    • Search results that used the entire corpus / total search results, including those that degraded gracefully.
    • “Stock check count” requests from product searches that used stock data fresher than 10 minutes / total stock check requests.
    • “Good user minutes” by some extended list of criteria / total user minutes.
  • Two properties make this shape worth insisting on:
    • It ranges 0% to 100%, where 0% means nothing works and 100% means nothing is broken. Intuitive, and it lends itself directly to a budget — the objective is a target percentage and the budget is 100% minus it.
    • Consistency buys you tooling. Alerting logic, analysis tools, budget calculation and reports can all expect the same three inputs: numerator, denominator, threshold.

The terms, each with its look-alike

TermPlain definitionConcrete exampleBoundary — what it gets confused with
SLI specification The assessment of service outcome you think matters to users, independent of how it is measured. “Ratio of home page requests that loaded in under 100 ms.” vs implementation: the specification is the question; the implementation is the instrument. One specification can have several implementations with genuinely different answers — which is why arguments about “our availability number” are usually arguments about implementation.
SLI implementation The specification plus a concrete way to measure it. The same ratio, measured from the latency column of the server log; or by probers running JavaScript in a browser in a VM; or by instrumentation in the page's own JavaScript reporting to a telemetry service. Each carries its own blind spot. Logs miss requests that never reach the backend. Probers catch unreachable-network errors but may miss issues affecting only a subset of users. Client instrumentation captures experience most accurately but needs code changes and new infrastructure — which then has its own reliability requirements.
Quality, coverage, cost The three axes an implementation trades on: how accurately it captures one customer's experience; how well it captures all customers' experience; and what it costs to build. Client-side instrumentation is high quality; a periodic prober is low coverage; server logs are cheap. Quality and coverage are different: an instrument can perfectly capture the experience of the 2% of users it watches. Confusing them is how teams end up confident in a number that speaks for almost nobody.
Request-driven A component where the user creates an event and expects a response. An HTTP service behind a browser; an API for a mobile app. Its indicators are availability, latency, quality — where quality means the proportion of responses served undegraded, which only matters if the service degrades gracefully rather than failing.
Pipeline A system that takes records in, mutates them, and puts the output somewhere else. Reading a relational database into a distributed hash table for serving; converting video formats; reading logs from many sources to generate reports; a monitoring system pulling metrics to generate time series and alerts. Its indicators are freshness, correctness, coverage. Note the range: this might be a simple real-time process on one instance, or a multistage batch job taking many hours — both are pipelines.
Storage A system that accepts data and makes it retrievable later. Bytes, records, files, videos. Its indicator is durability — and it carries a trap. If you hold a billion records over ten years but the user wants only today's, and today's are unavailable, they are unhappy even though almost all their data is readable. A durability number averaged over everything you store can be excellent and meaningless.

The worked example — a mobile phone game

  • The app on the phone talks to an HTTP API in the cloud, which writes state changes to permanent storage. A pipeline periodically runs over that data to generate league tables — high scores for today, this week, all time — writing to a separate league table store, served to both the mobile app and a website. Users upload custom avatars, used in-game and on the high-score site.
  • All three component types are present, so all seven indicator types are candidates. The guidance is to choose five or fewer indicator types representing the most critical functionality.
  • Availability and latency, API and web: success is based on HTTP status code — 5XX counts against, everything else counts as successful. Candidate sources: application server logs, load balancer monitoring, black-box monitoring, client-side instrumentation. The example picks load balancer monitoring, for two reasons worth remembering together: the metrics were already available, and they are closer to the user than the application server's logs.
  • Pipeline freshness: the pipeline records a watermark — the timestamp of when the data was updated. Two implementations: query the league table periodically counting fresh records against total, which treats every stale record as equally important regardless of how many users saw it; or have clients check the watermark on request and increment counters. The example picks the client-side version, because it correlates far more closely with user experience and is straightforward to add.
  • Pipeline coverage: the pipeline exports how many records it should have processed and how many it did. Known blind spot: it may miss records the pipeline never knew about because of misconfiguration.
  • Pipeline correctness: either inject data with known outputs and count how often output matches expectation, or compute correct output by a method distinct from the pipeline (likely more expensive, hence unsuitable for the pipeline itself) and sample input/output pairs. The example uses manually curated data with known good outputs, tested on every pipeline run — with the standing caveat that the curated data must be representative of real-world data or the indicator is representative of nothing.

Multiple grades for one indicator

A single threshold hides the tail. If 90% of requests return within 100 ms but the remaining 10% take ten seconds, many users are unhappy while the number looks fine. So set several thresholds on the same indicator: 90% of requests faster than 100 ms, and 99% faster than 400 ms. This applies to any indicator with a parameter that measures user unhappiness.

From measurement to a first objective

  • Measure what the service actually does, then round down to manageable numbers — roughly two significant figures of availability, and up to 50 ms of latency. (50 ms because users are unlikely to perceive a 50 ms change; the right window depends on the service — a reporting service is not a real-time game.)
  • Over four weeks the example API showed: 3,663,253 total requests; 3,557,865 successful (97.123%); 90th percentile latency 432 ms; 99th percentile 891 ms.
  • Rounded, that gives the proposed objectives and their budgets:
ObjectiveAllowed failures over four weeks
97% availability109,897

  • Note the deliberate consequence: if you set the objective from actual measurements, by design you met it. That is fine — the value is in what the distribution then tells you. Were there days in the past four weeks when the service missed it? Do those days correlate with real incidents? Was action taken, or should it have been?
  • On measuring latency: it is better to count the slow requests than to approximate them with a histogram — but if only histogram buckets are available, use them. Counting explicitly against configured thresholds is more accurate but needs more configuration, and makes changing the thresholds retroactively harder.
  • If you have no logs, metrics or historical performance at all, configure a source: for HTTP services a remote monitoring service doing a periodic ping or GET and reporting successes is a legitimate low-fidelity start.

Concept boundary — current performance as a starting point

The standing advice is not to set an objective from current performance, because it can commit you to unnecessarily strict targets.
But current performance is a good place to start if you have nothing else and a real process for iterating. The trap runs the other way too: don't let current performance limit you. Customers come to expect the service to perform as it does, so if you currently return successes 99.999% of the time in under 10 ms, any significant regression from that baseline may make them unhappy — whatever the published objective says.

ChoiceWhat it buysWhat it costs

Making it bite

Agreement, the policy, improving coverage, and deciding with the budget instead of arguing.

Tenth-grader version

  • A number only matters if people agreed to it beforehand and agreed what happens when it's missed.
  • Getting that agreement is also the best test of whether the number is any good — if nobody will sign, it's wrong.
  • Check your number against reality: when users complained, did your measurement notice?
  • And when your measurement and your users disagree, one of them needs changing — usually the measurement.

Start with the household budget

  • A household agrees to spend no more than a set amount each month. The number is easy. What makes it real is two other things: everyone agreed it in advance, and everyone agreed what happens if it's blown — no takeaways until next month.
  • The agreement is also the test. If one person refuses to sign because the number is impossible on their salary, the number is wrong, not the person.
  • And once a year you check it against reality: did the months you felt broke line up with the months you overspent? If they didn't, the budget is measuring the wrong thing.

Checking the number against reality

  • First you need a source of information about user satisfaction. The options are wide: count outages discovered manually, posts on public forums, support tickets, calls to customer service; attempt to measure sentiment on social media; add code to sample user happiness periodically; run face-to-face surveys. Start with something cheap to collect and iterate. Asking the product manager to fold reliability into their existing customer conversations about pricing and functionality is an excellent place to start.
  • Then correlate. Look at periods with a known outage — do they line up with steep drops in error budget? Look at times the indicator dipped — do they line up with known outages or a rise in support tickets? (If you're comfortable with statistics, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient quantifies this.)
  • The outliers are the interesting part. In the worked graph of tickets per day against budget lost, two points stand out: one day with only 5 tickets where 10% of the budget was lost, and one day with 40 tickets where no budget was lost at all. Both warrant closer investigation — the first is a precision problem, the second a recall problem.

Deciding with the budget

  • Size incidents by budget consumed. A release causing 100% NullPointerExceptions for four hours before revert produced 14,066 errors — against a budget of 109,897, that single event used 13%. A singly-homed state database failing, with 20 hours to restore from backups, caused an estimated 72,000 errors — 65% of the budget.
  • Then compare classes of incident, not instances. One server failure in five years, versus two or three bad releases needing rollback per year. On average, bad pushes cost twice as much error budget as database failures. The numbers say to address the release problem rather than investigate the server. That comparison is the entire point of the instrument.
  • In extremis, a team can declare an emergency with high-level approval to deprioritise all external demands until exit criteria are met — typically that the service is within objective and that steps have been taken to reduce the chance of a repeat: improving monitoring, improving testing, removing dangerous dependencies, or rearchitecting to remove known failure types.
  • And in the happy case: if the service runs flawlessly and needs little oversight, move it to a less hands-on tier of support. Keep incident response and high-level oversight; redirect the attention to systems that need it.

Concept boundary — two things the budget is honest about not knowing

Shape. The budget is an approximation of user satisfaction. A four-hour outage every 30 days probably produces fewer unhappy users than four separate one-hour outages, which in turn produces fewer than a constant 0.5% error rate — but the budget treats all three the same. Where that matters, you need thresholds tuned to the service.

Independence. It is tempting to do arithmetic: a service at 99.9% in one zone, deployed in two zones, “should” give 99.9999%. That assumes the two are wholly independent, which is almost never the case — common dependencies, common failure domains, shared fate, global control planes. Unless every one is enumerated and accounted for, any such calculation is deceptive.

Spending the budget to buy information

Canary and control, population and duration, metric selection — and the three things that look like canarying but aren't.

Tenth-grader version

  • Show the new version to a small slice of real users first, and compare them against everyone else.
  • If it's broken, only that slice suffers — and you find out for a fraction of the cost.
  • The comparison has to be against people using the system at the same time, not against yesterday.
  • And you have to pick the right things to watch, or you'll either miss real breakage or cry wolf until everyone ignores you.

Start with the coal mine

  • Miners carried a canary underground. The bird is smaller and breathes faster than a human, so it is intoxicated by dangerous gases faster than its handlers — the warning arrives while there is still time to act.
  • The bird isn't a test of the mine in general. It's a small, fast, expendable sample of the same air everyone else is breathing.
  • Two things make it work: the bird is in the real mine, not a model of one, and there are miners breathing the same air right now to compare it against.
  • Take either away and you have something else: a laboratory experiment, or yesterday's air quality reading.
Canarying is “a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation.” The part receiving the change is the canary; the remainder is the control. Canarying is effectively an A/B testing process.

The terms, each with its look-alike

TermPlain definitionConcrete exampleBoundary — what it gets confused with
Canary / control The subset receiving the change, and the remainder that doesn't. 5% of production on the release candidate; 95% on the live version. The canary is usually a much smaller subset of production, or affects a much smaller subset of users. The fraction of total load you run on the canary should be proportional to the size of the canary population — otherwise you're testing a different system, not a smaller one.
Before/after evaluation Replace the old system entirely, then compare behaviour before and after the change. A blue/green cutover evaluated by comparing this week to last. One might call it “a canary deployment in time-space” — you segment by time rather than by population. Risky because time is one of the biggest sources of change in observed metrics: release on Monday and you may be comparing a business day to a weekend, with users behaving completely differently. The degradation you see may have happened to the control too.
Gradual canary Multiple stages of increasing population, rather than one evaluation. A small first stage, then larger ones once it passes. The point isn't just safety — it's metric selection. In the first stage you have no confidence, so you use only the clearest indications of a problem: application crashes, request failures. Later stages have enough population to make noisier metrics meaningful.
Blue/green deployment Two instances: green serving traffic, blue ready to serve. Deploy to blue, then move traffic. Cutover without downtime; rollback is a trivial reversal of the router change. Costs twice the resources. And used as normally described, it is a before/after canary, with all that implies. It becomes a real A/B canary only if you run both simultaneously and split traffic — green as control, blue as canary, population controlled by the traffic split.
Artificial load generation Driving the candidate with synthetic traffic instead of real users. Load tests in QA, preproduction, or even production. Not canarying by definition, though still useful. Good at code coverage, poor at state coverage — especially hard in mutable systems with caches, cookies and request affinity — and it may not model organic traffic shifts. Also genuinely dangerous in some systems: synthetic load on a billing system might send callouts to card providers and actually charge customers.
Traffic teeing Copy real traffic to both systems; production serves and responds, the canary serves the copy and discards the responses. You can even compare canary responses to real ones for further analysis. Representative traffic, but more complicated to set up, and it doesn't identify risk in stateful systems: if the canary and production share a cache, an artificially inflated hit rate invalidates your performance measurements.

Release engineering, the foundation underneath

  • Five principles: reproducible builds (same inputs, same artifacts, this week as last); automated builds (check in code, automation produces and uploads artifacts); automated tests; automated deployments (performed by computers, not humans); small deployments (artifacts contain small, self-contained changes).
  • What they buy: less operational load; enforced peer review and version control, since automation is code; consistent repeatable processes with fewer mistakes; and a pipeline you can monitor and measure — how long does a release take to reach production, how often are releases successful, what would catch defects earlier, what can be parallelised.
  • The virtuous cycle: automation lets you release more often → fewer changes bundled per artifact → cheaper and easier rollback → and bug fixes reach users faster.
  • A related lever: separate components that change at different rates — binaries, environments like the JVM, kernel and OS, libraries, service config and flags, feature and experiment config, user config. Feature flag frameworks let you separate feature launches from binary releases, so a binary carrying several features can enable them one at a time, and selectively disable just the misbehaving ones without waiting for the next build cycle.

The arithmetic that justifies the whole practice

  • Take a release candidate that fails 20% of requests. Deployed globally, you fail 20% of everything, immediately.
  • Deploy it to a 5% canary population instead: 20% errors on 5% of traffic gives a 1% overall error rate. Same defect, same detection and rollback time — a twentieth of the damage.
  • The general statement: impact on the budget is directly proportional to the amount of traffic exposed to defects. That is the sentence to remember.
  • But notice what the arithmetic hides — the errors become hard to see. At 1% overall the canary's failure may be indistinguishable from background noise, which is why metric breakdown by version matters (below).

The model's assumptions, stated honestly

That calculation assumes uniform load; that you may spend your entire error budget on canaries; that only unavailability introduced by new releases counts, rather than actual availability; and a 100% failure rate as the worst case, when real defects rarely affect all usage. It also allows overall availability to dip below the objective for the canary's duration.

“Essentially, all models are wrong, but some are useful.” Use the simplest model that meets your technical and business objectives — chasing technical correctness here leads to overinvestment in modelling, and for complex services to incessant tuning for no real benefit.

Choosing population and duration

  • Development velocity sets the ceiling. Release weekly and you can afford fairly long canaries. Release daily and a week-long canary is impossible. Deploy continuously — twenty times a day — and the duration must be significantly shorter.
  • Run one canary at a time. Simultaneous canaries add significant mental effort to track system state, which becomes a real problem in exactly the nonstandard circumstances where reasoning quickly matters — and overlapping canaries risk signal contamination. The advice is strong: run only one.
  • Four dimensions to balance, and they can be mutually at odds:
DimensionWhat it demands
Size and durationBig enough and long enough to be representative. Terminating after a handful of queries gives no useful signal for systems with diverse queries. The higher the processing rate, the less time you need to be confident the behaviour is attributable to the change rather than a random artifact.
Traffic volumeEnough traffic that the system has handled a representative sample and had a chance to react badly to it. The more homogeneous the requests, the less volume you need.
Time of dayPerformance defects typically manifest only under heavy load — resource contention, database write conflicts, locking in multithreaded applications. Deploying off-peak likely won't trigger them.
Metrics to evaluateTrivial metrics like query success evaluate quickly; something like queue depth may need more time or a larger population to give a clear signal.

Once you have metrics from past canaries, you can choose parameters based on typical canary evaluation failure rates rather than hypothetical worst cases.

Choosing metrics — where most canary processes go wrong

  • The metric must indicate problems. Start from your indicators: good ones already have strong attribution to service health, and you've done the work. A failed user request is probably a problem — though be disciplined, since a request can fail because the user asked for something unreasonable, like a nonexistent URL.
  • More metrics is not better. Each needs a defined notion of acceptable behaviour. Too strict and you get false positives — you'll believe a good canary is bad. Too loose and bad canaries go undetected. Getting it right is time-consuming and needs analysis, and expectations must be re-evaluated as the service evolves.
  • So stack-rank metrics by how well they indicate user-perceivable problems and take the top few — perhaps no more than a dozen. Beyond that, returns diminish and are outweighed by maintenance cost, or by the damage to trust in the release process when they aren't maintained.
  • For the example frontend service: candidate metrics include CPU usage, memory footprint, HTTP return codes, latency, correctness. The best are HTTP return codes and latency, because their degradation maps most closely to a problem that impacts users. CPU usage is a poor choice — increased resource usage doesn't necessarily impact the service, and produces a flaky, noisy canary process, which leads to the canary being disabled or ignored by operators, defeating the point of having one.
  • The tricky case: HTTP 404. It can mean the user followed a wrong URL — imagine a broken link shared on a popular discussion board — or that the server incorrectly stopped serving a resource. The workaround is to exclude 400-level codes from canary evaluation and add black-box monitoring testing for the presence of a particular URL, then feed that into the analysis. This isolates the canary process from odd user behaviour.

Representative and attributable — the isolation problem

  • In a large population you will have outliers: oversubscribed machines, machines on different kernels with different performance characteristics, machines on an overloaded network segment. So the difference between canary and control is as much a function of the two infrastructures as of the change you deployed.
  • Isolate the metric itself. Whole-system CPU is poor — database load and log rotation move it too. Better: CPU time spent by the process serving the request. Better still: CPU time serving the request over the window the process was actually scheduled on a CPU. An oversubscribed neighbour is a real problem and monitoring should catch it, but it isn't caused by your change and shouldn't fail your canary.
  • Tie the metric back to indicators. If a metric can change wildly with no impact on the service, it is unlikely to make a good canary metric.
  • Shared failure domains cut both ways. A bad canary can negatively impact the control; bad system behaviour can make you misjudge the canary. Two consecutive requests from one client can cross the boundary — the canary's response changes the content of the second request, which lands on the control and alters its behaviour. So a stop signal does not necessarily mean the canary is at fault, and you should also use absolute measures, such as defined objectives, to confirm the system is operating correctly.

What monitoring has to be able to do

  • Fine-grained breakdown. A 5% canary at a 20% error rate shows up as 1% overall — potentially indistinguishable from other sources of error. Broken down by population servicing the request, the same data shows the release candidate serving the vast majority of errors while the live version sits at a low steady state. Monitoring that reasons well about a whole service is not sufficient to analyse a canary.
  • Matching intervals. Canaries are time-limited by design, so an aggregated metric can wreck the evaluation. The failure runs: an unrelated event causes some errors; a canary deploys to 5% for 30 minutes; the system evaluates the errors-per-hour metric; the deployment is judged bad because errors-per-hour differs from the control. The metric was computed over an hour to judge a 30-minute deployment. Make metric intervals the same as, or less than, the canary duration.

Canarying things that aren't request/response

  • Duration follows the work unit. For interactive systems we assume processing takes seconds, far shorter than the canary. For rendering pipelines or video encoding it can take much longer, so the canary duration must at minimum span a single work unit.
  • Isolation gets harder. Many pipelines have one work assigner and a fleet of workers; in multistage pipelines a unit is processed, returned to the pool, then picked up again. Ensure the workers processing a particular unit always come from the same pool — canary or control — or the signals get increasingly mixed.
  • Metrics get harder. You may care about end-to-end time to process the unit (the analogue of latency) as well as the quality of the processing itself, which is entirely application-specific.

Concept boundary — what makes something a canary

It is not simply “a careful rollout”, and it is not any comparison that produces a graph.
It is a partial and time-limited deployment evaluated against a concurrent control carrying real traffic. Remove the concurrency and you have before/after evaluation, where time itself is the biggest confounder. Remove the real traffic and you have load testing, which gives good code coverage and poor state coverage. Both are useful; neither tells you what a canary tells you.

Buy information at a price you set. The defect rate is not yours to choose, but the exposure is — and cost to your budget is directly proportional to the traffic you expose. Section 9 — keep covered until you've written your own answers

Answer key

Part 1 · Q1 — the proud 100% team

(a) Three costs. Quarterly releases — holding perfection means you can never change, and change is the number one source of outages, so the promise is being paid for in stagnation. An exhausted rota — at 100% you can only ever react to the sub-100% that is guaranteed to happen, which is an operations posture rather than an engineering one. Unbounded spend on reliability — each additional nine costs more while the marginal utility to customers approaches zero, and users can't perceive it anyway through the chain of systems between you and them.

(b) Because the record is evidence for the wrong proposition. Eleven minutes of downtime proves the target is nearly achievable, which makes it feel like a working policy rather than a cost. Nobody sees the counterfactual — the features not shipped, the engineers not sleeping, the improvements not attempted. Success at a bad target is the hardest kind of argument to win, because the damage is entirely in what didn't happen.

(c) Not the number — ownership. The objective must be owned by someone empowered to trade feature velocity against reliability; in a small organisation the CTO, more usually the product owner or product manager. Until someone with that authority owns it, any number you pick will lose every argument it has with a launch date. Credit also for naming the accompanying precondition: agreement that a target below 100% is acceptable at all.

Part 1 · Q2 — the 99.5% that nothing follows from

(a) They have an objective without teeth — the third maturity level. What's missing is a committed error budget policy, so the objective functions as a KPI or reporting metric rather than a decision-making tool.

(b) Precondition 3 is plainly unmet: the organisation has not committed to using the budget for decision making and prioritising, formalised in a policy. Precondition 1 is almost certainly unmet too — a number published on a wiki is not evidence that all stakeholders approved it as fit for the product. Precondition 4, a process for refining the objective, shows no sign of existing either. Any single failure is sufficient because the preconditions are conjunctive: without all four you cannot adopt an error-budget-based approach, and compliance degrades to reporting.

(c) An error budget policy, approved by the product manager, the development team and the team defending the service. It must specify at least: the actions taken when the budget is exhausted and who takes them; the authors, reviewers and approvers with approval and next-review dates; and a clear escalation path if there is disagreement about the calculation or whether the actions are appropriate. The near-miss fix — publishing the number more prominently — changes nothing, because visibility was never the constraint. Nobody was empowered to act, and a more visible number that still causes nothing to happen is just a better-lit dashboard.

Part 2 · Q1 — 99.95% from the logs, complaints from one carrier

(a) The specification — the proportion of user requests that succeed — is probably right; the implementation is the problem. Server logs can only record requests that arrived, so requests that never reached the backend are invisible. A carrier-specific network problem produces exactly this signature: users fail, the log-derived number stays beautiful, and the two facts are not in conflict because they are measuring different populations.

(b) Black-box probers executing JavaScript in a browser in a VM: these catch errors when requests can't reach your network, but may miss issues affecting only a subset of users — and a prober fleet that doesn't sit on that carrier will miss this one too. Client-side instrumentation in the page's own JavaScript reporting to a telemetry service: this captures the real user experience most accurately, including failures that never reach you. Load balancer monitoring is worth naming as the cheap intermediate — closer to the user than app logs, often already available — though it still won't see a request that never arrived.

(c) Adopt load balancer monitoring first if you have it, because it is cheap and strictly better than logs, then work toward client-side instrumentation. The extra obligation of the most accurate option is that the telemetry infrastructure has its own reliability requirements — you now depend on a new system to know whether your system is working, and you must decide what you believe when it fails.

Part 2 · Q2 — the nightly recommendations job

(a) It's a pipeline. Its three indicator types are freshness, correctness and coverage. What the team implemented is none of them cleanly — “runs that completed without error” is closest to an availability measure of the job itself, and it tells you the process exited zero, not that users got good data.

(b) “Days out of date” is freshness: have the pipeline record a watermark with the timestamp of the last update, and have clients check it when they request data, incrementing one counter for the request and another when the data was fresher than the threshold. That weights the measure by who actually saw stale data. “Plain wrong” is correctness: either inject data with known outputs and count how often the output matches, or compute correct output by a separate, more expensive method and sample input/output pairs.

(c) Coverage is measured by exporting the number of records the pipeline should have processed against the number it did — and its retained blind spot is that it cannot count records it never knew about, typically through misconfiguration. The standing caveat on correctness by curated test data is that the curated data must be representative of real-world data; if it isn't, the indicator is representative of nothing, however green it looks. And the near-miss fix fails for a structural reason: tightening the existing objective changes nothing, because “runs completed without error” measures the job's exit status, not the data. It has been passing at 100% for a month while both complaints were true, so no threshold on that indicator can ever surface staleness or wrongness — you need different indicators, not a stricter one.

Section 9 continued

Part 3 · Q1 — checkout meets its number and feels flaky

(a) A recall problem: significantly user-impacting events are occurring and the indicator isn't capturing them. Precision is not the issue — nothing is alerting falsely; the instrument is silent when it should speak.

(b) Because per-request availability measures individual HTTP requests, and a multi-step flow can fail while every constituent request succeeds — the payment step can return a well-formed response that doesn't complete a purchase, or the user can be dropped between steps. Aggregate per-request success is a poor proxy for “did the user achieve their goal.” The concept that addresses it is the critical user journey: a sequence of tasks that is a core part of the user's experience — for shopping, searching for a product, adding to a cart, completing a purchase.

(c) The attractive property is that critical user journeys improve recall without affecting precision — you catch more real user harm without generating more false alarms, which is exactly the trade you normally can't get. The practical difficulty is measurement: these tasks almost certainly won't map to existing indicators, each involves multiple complex steps that can fail anywhere, and inferring success or failure from logs is extremely difficult — you cannot easily distinguish a user who failed at step three from one who simply got distracted and left. You'd measure it by joining distinct log events, advanced JavaScript probing, or client-side instrumentation. The near-miss fix — tightening the per-request availability objective — cannot work here: every individual request in the flow already succeeded, so an indicator sitting at 100% for these journeys will still sit at 100% however strict the target. The failure happens between requests, and no threshold on a per-request measure reaches it.

Part 3 · Q2 — someone else's outage, your budget

(a) Two schools of thought, and both are honest. One: your team shouldn't halt releases or divert to reliability work, because your system didn't cause the issue — punishing you for it is arbitrary and demoralising. Two: enact the freeze regardless of cause, to minimise the chance of further outages while the service is already degraded. The guidance favours the second, on a single ground: it will make your users happier. Users experienced an unreliable service; they neither know nor care which team owns the failing component, and stacking your own risk on top of a bad month is what they'd least want. Flexibility is allowed — depending on the nature of the outage and dependency, freezing may not be practical.

(b) This belongs in the documented error budget policy, agreed in advance and recorded for posterity. Deciding now is the wrong time because the question has become a negotiation between people with immediate stakes in the answer — the development lead is arguing a position, not a principle. The general rule holds here: if a service exhausts its budget and stakeholders don't agree the policy should apply, you don't argue it in the moment, you return to the policy approval stage.

(c) Structurally: the identity service is a critical dependency — its unavailability makes yours unavailable — so its reliability guarantee must be at least as high as the guarantee of the action depending on it, and the team running it must own that objective the way you own yours. If it can't reach that level, engineer around it: use a different component, or add defences. Two named defences: caching (so a short outage is invisible), and graceful degradation (serve a reduced experience rather than failing). Offline store-and-forward processing also earns credit.

Part 4 · Q1 — twenty deploys a day, eight metrics, endless re-runs

(a) Duration: six hours is incompatible with twenty deployments a day. Canary duration must factor in development velocity, and with continuous deployment it has to be significantly shorter — otherwise canaries queue, or you're forced into simultaneous canaries, which add real mental load and risk signal contamination. Only one canary should be in flight at a time. Population: 2% is small enough that slower or noisier metrics may never produce a clear signal in the time available; representativeness depends on the canary being sizeable enough and running long enough, and they have squeezed both.

(b) The re-running is a false positive problem: with eight metrics, several of which can move without any user impact — host CPU, memory footprint, thread count, cache hit ratio, file descriptors — the acceptable-behaviour definitions are collectively too strict, and canaries fail for reasons unrelated to the change. It is more dangerous than a canary that misses defects because it destroys the authority of the process: a flaky canary gets disabled or ignored by operators, which defeats the point of having one. A canary with poor recall still stops some bad releases; a canary nobody believes stops none, and has consumed the budget for building one.

(c) Start from your service level indicators — good ones already have strong attribution to service health, and the work is done — then stack-rank metrics by how well they indicate actual user-perceivable problems and keep only the top few, perhaps no more than a dozen but here far fewer. Keep error rate (HTTP return codes) and p99 latency: degradation in either maps closely to real user harm. Drop host CPU, memory, threads, cache hit ratio and file descriptors as primary signals — an increase in resource usage doesn't necessarily impact the service. Credit for noting that whole-host CPU is doubly bad because it isn't isolated: other processes move it, so a better metric would be CPU time spent by the process serving the request.

Part 4 · Q2 — the transcoding pipeline that passed

(a) Duration shorter than a work unit: twenty minutes cannot evaluate an encoder whose long-file work units take longer than that, so the failures that mattered had not finished — let alone been measured — when the canary was promoted. Canary duration must at minimum span the duration of a single work unit. Metric selection wrong for the system: they watched an error rate, but the relevant signals for a pipeline are end-to-end time to process the work unit and the quality of the processing, which is application-specific — corrupted output is a correctness failure that an error rate will never show, because the job succeeded.

(b) Because service-wide aggregation dilutes the canary until it disappears. The standard illustration: a canary at 5% of the population running at a 20% error rate produces an overall error rate of just 1%, which may be indistinguishable from other sources of error. Here at 10% the dilution is smaller but the principle identical — and since the defect produced no errors at all, the aggregate was doubly useless. You must be able to perform fine-grained breakdowns that differentiate metrics between the canary and control populations. Monitoring that reasons well about an entire service is not sufficient to analyse a canary.

(c) In multistage pipelines a work unit is processed by a worker, returned to the pool, then picked up for the next stage — possibly by a different worker. The requirement is that the workers processing a particular unit are always pulled from the same pool, canary or control. Without it the signals get increasingly mixed: a unit partly processed by each population produces a result attributable to neither, so you cannot tell whether the candidate caused what you're seeing. And the obvious response to “we saw nothing” — enlarging the canary population — would not have helped, because neither cause was a sample-size problem. The duration was shorter than a work unit, so the relevant work hadn't finished no matter how many workers ran it; and the metric watched cannot see output that is corrupt but successful, at any population. More workers would have produced more unmeasured corruption, which is not a signal.

How to score yourself

Two marks, both quick. Completeness: did you name every element, and — for this competency especially — did you say who has to agree and what it costs? Answers that describe a mechanism but skip the negotiation are half answers here. Phrasing: could you have said it in a review without hand-waving? If you wrote “the monitoring is wrong” where the key says “the specification is right but the implementation can't see requests that never reached the backend,” the idea is there and the mechanism isn't — and the mechanism is what changes anyone's mind.
