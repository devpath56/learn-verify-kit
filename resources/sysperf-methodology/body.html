Section 1

Learning goal & roadmap

Learning goal — what you should be able to do when you close this

Handed an unfamiliar system that is “slow”, with no idea where to start, you can:

  1. Ask the six questions that define the problem before you log in to anything.
  2. Choose a method — and say which situation it is the answer to, rather than reaching for the tool you happen to know.
  3. Quantify what you find, so you can rank it against everything else and know when to stop.
  4. Distrust the statistic in front of you: name what an average hides, what a five-minute interval hides, and what a percentile will not tell you about a bimodal distribution.

The core model in one paragraph

You can learn every metric a system emits and still be unable to fix anything, because the hard part is not the definitions — it is the procedure for moving from signals to solutions. That procedure has four layers. Vocabulary comes first, because a word with two meanings will send you to the wrong place: utilization means two different things, and one of them stops being a warning exactly when you need it. Then posture: analysis can start from the resources or from the requests, and the perspective you inherit from whatever documentation you read is not the only one. Then method: a small number of procedures — check every resource, check every service, characterise the input, split the latency in half — that tell you where to start and what to do next, as opposed to the anti-methods, whose common signature is a hypothesis with no data behind it. Then the numbers, which are the last place you can still fool yourself: averages that describe nothing that happened, intervals that hide the seconds that mattered, and a mean response time that has already doubled once utilization passes 60%.

Tenth-grader version — the whole thing at once

  • Knowing what all the dials mean doesn't tell you which dial to look at. That's the whole problem.
  • Some words are traps. “Busy all the time” and “can't take any more” sound the same and aren't.
  • You can look at the machine's parts, or at the work being asked of it. Most people only ever learn one of those.
  • The bad ways of investigating all look the same from outside: someone has a theory and no evidence for it.
  • And when you finally have numbers, the numbers can still mislead you — especially averages.

The words decide the measurement

Six terms, two of which mean different things to different people — including one that means two things at once.

Tenth-grader version

  • Latency is waiting. Response time is waiting plus doing. People use the first word for both, so always say what you measured.
  • Utilization has two meanings: “how much of the time was it busy” and “how much of what it could do is it doing”. Those come apart badly.
  • A lift can be moving 100% of the time and still have room for more people. A disk can too.
  • Saturation is work piling up because it can't be taken yet. Any amount of it is a problem, because it is time someone spent waiting.
  • And systems don't get slow gradually — they scale fine, and then there's a bend in the curve where they stop.

The everyday version

A lift in a busy building. It is busy — moving between floors — 100% of the time, all afternoon. That is time-based utilization, and it is at maximum. But it is not full: it can still take more passengers on each trip. That is capacity-based utilization, and it is nowhere near maximum. Now people start waiting in the lobby because every arriving lift is full: that is saturation, and the moment it begins, someone's day contains waiting that wasn't there before. Notice that a monitoring dashboard reporting “100% busy” would have looked identical all afternoon, both before and after the queue formed.

The terms, each with its look-alike

TermPlain definitionConcrete exampleBoundary — what it gets confused with
Latency A measure of the time an operation spends waiting to be serviced. In some contexts it is used for the entire operation time. A page load is DNS latency, then TCP connection latency (the handshake only), then TCP data transfer time — and all three together may be called the latency of something at a higher level. vs response time, which spans the waiting and the servicing and the transfer of the result. Because the single word is ambiguous, always qualify it: request latency, connection latency, query latency. Naming the target of the measurement is not pedantry; it is the difference between two different numbers.
Why convert to latency Latency is a time, so different problems can be compared, ranked, and their speedups predicted. Choosing between 100 network operations and 50 disk operations is a hard question with many factors. Choosing between 100 ms of network I/O and 50 ms of disk I/O is not. vs IOPS or throughput, which cannot accurately be compared across components or used to calculate a predicted speedup. Where you can convert a metric to time, do — it is what makes prioritisation possible at all.
Utilization (time-based) U = B/T: the fraction of an observation period during which the resource was busy. Formally defined in queueing theory. This is what operating system tools report — a disk monitoring tool calls it percent busy, which conveys the underlying measurement better than the word “utilization” does. vs capacity-based utilization, below. Time-based tells you how busy something is, and near 100% performance can degrade badly under contention — but for a resource that can work on several things at once, it may not degrade at all.
Utilization (capacity-based) The proportion of the throughput it could deliver that it is currently delivering. The capacity-planning sense of the word. A disk at 100% capacity utilization cannot accept more work. A disk at 100% time-based utilization only means it was busy the whole interval. 100% busy does not mean 100% capacity. A storage array frequently runs at 100% because some disk is always busy, while plenty of disks sit idle and it can take more work. Ideally you would measure both; usually you cannot, because it would require knowing what the device's controller is doing and predicting its capacity.
Saturation The degree to which more work is requested of a resource than it can process. Also called pressure. Begins at 100% capacity-based utilization, and in the idealised picture increases linearly with load beyond it. Measured as a wait-queue length, or as time spent waiting. vs utilization, which is about busyness. Any degree of saturation is a performance issue, because it is time spent waiting — that is latency by another name. Under the time-based definition, queueing may begin well before 100%, or not at 100% at all, depending on how much parallel work the resource accepts.
Knee point The boundary where a system's throughput stops scaling linearly with load and contention begins to degrade it. It may occur when a component reaches 100% utilization — the saturation point — or as it approaches 100% and queueing becomes frequent and significant. Beyond it, throughput can actually decrease as load increases, because contention and coherency overheads mean less real work gets done. The same curve appears if you scale a resource instead of the load.
Load vs architecture Two distinct causes of the same symptom. An architecture problem is how the work is done; a load problem is that there is too much of it. Architecture: a single-threaded application busy on one CPU while others sit idle; or many threads contending for one lock. Load: an application busy on all CPUs with requests still queueing. The distinction decides the fix. If analysis shows queueing but no problem with how the work is performed, the issue is load — and in a cloud environment that is the point at which you add instances rather than change code.
Cache hit ratio hits / (hits + misses). Higher is better, and the relationship to performance is strongly non-linear. The improvement from 98% to 99% is far greater than from 10% to 11%, because the two storage tiers differ so much in speed. The greater the difference, the steeper the curve. vs the miss rate (misses per second), which is proportional to the penalty and often easier to interpret. A 90% hit ratio at 200 misses/s may be worse than an 80% ratio at 20 misses/s — ten times fewer slow reads. To be certain: runtime = (hit rate × hit latency) + (miss rate × miss latency).
Cache warmth Cold: empty or full of unwanted data, hit ratio near zero. Warm: useful data, but not a high enough ratio to be hot. Hot: commonly requested data and a high hit ratio — over 99%, for example. Retention and eviction policies decide what stays: most/least recently used, most/least frequently used, and cheaper approximations of them. The trap is how long warming takes. A storage appliance with 128 GB of memory cache and 600 GB of flash, fed by disks delivering 2,000 reads/s at 8 KB, warms at only 16 MB/s — over two hours for the memory cache and over ten hours for the flash. A benchmark shorter than that is measuring a cold cache.

Three habits the vocabulary is there to support

  • Tune closest to where the work is done. Tuning at the application level can eliminate work entirely and improve performance by a large factor — of the order of 20×. Tuning down at the storage device level, after the higher-level code has already been executed, may improve the result by percentages — of the order of 20%. But note the separate point: the most effective level to tune is not always the most effective level from which to observe. A slow query may be best understood from its time on-CPU or from the file system and disk I/O it causes, both of which are visible from operating system tools.
  • Treat every tuning recommendation as perishable. Performance characteristics change with users, hardware, and software versions — a system limited by its network today may be limited by disk or CPU after an upgrade. A tunable value found on the internet may be right, may have been right once, or may be a temporary workaround for a bug since fixed. Browsing such advice is still useful for discovering which parameters exist and have needed changing; your job is then to decide whether they apply here. Keep changed tunables in version control with the reasons.
  • Know why you are stopping. Three defensible reasons: you have explained the bulk of the problem — if a 3× slowdown is accounted for by something worth 12% of CPU, you have not; the potential return is now less than the cost of the analysis; or there is a bigger return elsewhere. All three require you to have quantified what you found.

Concept boundary — the three states of your own knowledge

Known-knowns: you know to check the metric, and you know its value.
Known-unknowns: you know the metric or subsystem exists, and you have not looked. You know you could profile what is making the CPUs busy; you have not.
Unknown-unknowns: you do not know the thing exists, so you are not checking it — you may not know that device interrupts can become heavy CPU consumers.
Why it matters: the more you learn, the more unknown-unknowns become known-unknowns, which are things you can now go and check. Writing down what you could not measure is therefore a real finding, not a gap in your notes.

Where you stand decides what you see

Two perspectives, three anti-methods with one shared tell, and the procedures that replace them.

Tenth-grader version

  • You can investigate a slow system by looking at its parts — processors, memory, disks — or by looking at the work being asked of it.
  • Most documentation teaches the first one, so most people don't realise the second exists.
  • The bad ways of investigating all share one feature: someone has a theory and no evidence for it.
  • The cheapest good move is to just ask six questions before touching anything. Problems get solved this way without anyone logging in.
  • After that, work like a scientist: guess, predict what the guess implies, test it, and let the data kill bad guesses fast.

The everyday version

A doctor with a patient who feels unwell. One approach is to work through the body's systems in turn — heart, lungs, bloods — which is thorough and belongs to whoever is responsible for the machinery. The other is to start from the complaint: when does it hurt, what were you doing, what changed. Both are legitimate and they find different things. What is not legitimate is the version where the doctor announces “it's probably your diet” before asking a single question and refers you to a dietician — and, when that comes back clear, announces it is probably your sleep. That is not a diagnosis; it is a hypothesis with nothing behind it, and it costs other people's time.

The terms, each with its look-alike

TermPlain definitionConcrete exampleBoundary — what it gets confused with
Resource analysis Bottom-up. Begin with the system resources: processors, memory, disks, network interfaces, buses, interconnects. Used for investigating whether a type of resource is responsible, and for capacity planning. Best-suited metrics: IOPS, throughput, utilization, saturation. vs the assumption that it is the perspective. It is common partly because the widely available documentation teaches it — the operating system “stat” tools. Reading that documentation, remember it is one perspective, not the only one. It is typically performed by whoever is responsible for the physical resources.
Workload analysis Top-down. Examine the load applied and how the application is responding. Three targets: requests (the load applied), latency (the response time), and completion (looking for errors). Best-suited metrics: throughput and latency. vs resource analysis, which starts at the other end of the stack. Note the third target: a request may complete quickly and with an error status that causes a retry, accumulating latency invisibly. And the fastest query is still the one you don't do at all — examining request attributes can identify work to eliminate even when latency is fine.
Streetlight anti-method The absence of a deliberate method: choose the observability tools you are familiar with, or find at random, and see if anything obvious shows up. Named for the drunk searching for keys under the streetlight — not because he lost them there, but because that is where the light is best. vs the tools method, which is the systematic version of the same limitation. The specific danger here is not that it finds nothing: it is that what it finds may be an issue but not the issue, and nothing in the approach quantifies the finding well enough to tell.
Random change anti-method Experimental guessing: pick something to change, change it one way, measure; change it the other way, measure; keep whichever beat the baseline; repeat. It can eventually find tuning that works for the tested workload. vs a controlled experiment. It is very time-consuming, and it leaves behind tuning nobody understood in the first place — possibly working around a bug that is later fixed, after which the tuning stays and no longer makes sense. The other risk is a poorly understood change causing a worse problem at peak load.
Blame-someone-else anti-method Find a component you are not responsible for, hypothesise that it is the cause, redirect the issue to that team, and when proven wrong, repeat. “Maybe it's the network — can someone check with the network team?” The identifying feature, and the one worth memorising, is a lack of data leading to the hypothesis. The countermeasure is procedural: ask the accuser for the screenshots showing which tools were run and how the output was interpreted, and take those to someone else for a second opinion.
Ad hoc checklist method Stepping through a canned list of checks, each with an interpretation and a fix. Common in support, often under time pressure. An entry looks like: run this tool, read this column; if the value is consistently over this threshold under load, then the disk is slow or overloaded. Not an anti-method exactly: it delivers the most value in the shortest time, and it is an effective way to make sure a whole team can check for common issues. But every entry is a point-in-time recommendation that decays, and checklists skew toward issues with easily documented fixes — tunable settings — rather than custom fixes to code or environment.
Problem statement Six questions asked before anything else, which is why it should be the first method you attempt on a new issue. What makes you think there is a problem? Has it ever performed well? What changed recently — software, hardware, load? Can it be expressed as latency or runtime? Does it affect others? What is the environment, with versions and configuration? vs starting with tools. Asking and answering these often points straight at the cause: performance issues have been solved this way alone, over the phone, without logging in to a server or looking at a single metric.
Scientific method Question, hypothesis, prediction, test, analysis. The question is the problem statement; the test may be observational or experimental. Observational: hypothesise noisy neighbours are contending for disk I/O; predict file system latency will dominate query latency; trace it and find it is under 5%; conclude the file system and disks are not responsible. Note what that example achieves: the issue is unsolved, and large parts of the environment have been ruled out. Also note the discipline for experimental tests — if the change doesn't fix it, reverse it before forming a new hypothesis, because changing several things at once makes it impossible to tell which mattered.
Negative test Deliberately making performance worse to learn about the system. Suspecting that managing a larger cache costs compute, progressively shrink the record size so more records are needed for the same data, and predict performance degrades progressively. vs an experiment that tries to help. Often the easier experiment: artificially reducing a cache is usually cheaper than adding memory, and a confirmed prediction is evidence either way.
Diagnosis cycle hypothesis → instrumentation → data → hypothesis. A loop rather than a line. Like a doctor running a series of small tests, refining the hypothesis after each. vs the scientific method, which it closely resembles. The emphasis differs: the cycle stresses that data leads quickly to the next hypothesis. Both have a good balance of theory and data, and the operative advice is the same — get to data fast, so bad theories are identified early and discarded.
Tools method List the tools available, list the useful metrics each provides, list how to interpret each metric. The result is a prescriptive checklist. Fairly effective in practice: it does identify certain resource bottlenecks and errors, though not always efficiently. vs a method that starts from questions. It relies exclusively on the tools you have or know, which gives an incomplete view — and, worse, the user is unaware the view is incomplete and may remain so. Issues needing custom tooling may never be found. It also scales badly: many tools with overlapping functionality cost time to evaluate.

Concept boundary — the anti-methods are not stupid, they are untethered

What the three anti-methods have in common is not laziness. It is that the hypothesis arrives before the data, or without any data at all — which is stated explicitly as the identifying feature of blame-someone-else and is equally true of the other two. The ad hoc checklist is deliberately not among them: it is a real method, and its weakness is decay rather than untetheredness.
What separates the good methods is not sophistication either. The problem statement is six questions and no tools; the diagnosis cycle is four words. What they share is that data comes first and the finding gets quantified, which is what lets you rule things out, rank what you found, and know when to stop.
The test to apply to yourself: what did I observe before I formed this theory, and how big is the thing I think I have found?

The methods worth running

A small number of procedures that answer “where do I start” for different shapes of problem.

Tenth-grader version

  • For every part of the machine, ask three things: how busy, how much waiting, how many errors. That's one method and it covers a lot.
  • For every service, ask three different things: how many requests, how many failed, how long they took.
  • To find work that shouldn't exist at all, look at what's being asked of the system rather than at the system.
  • To find where the time goes, split the time in two, follow the bigger half, and repeat.
  • And check the boring stuff: is anything just misconfigured? That check takes minutes and nobody remembers to do it.

The everyday version

A restaurant kitchen where the food is coming out late. One approach walks the stations — grill, fryer, pass — and asks of each: how busy is it, is there a queue building, is anything broken? That is a complete sweep of the machinery and it takes minutes. Another approach stands at the pass and asks of the orders: how many are coming in, how many are being sent back, how long are they taking? That is the diners' view, and it tells you whether the problem is that the kitchen got slower or that more people walked in. And before either, there is the check nobody does: with the kitchen empty, is the grill actually on the right setting, and is one of the two fryers still switched off from last night?

The USE method For every resource, check utilization, saturation and errors. Used early in an investigation to find systemic bottlenecks. Utilization as a percentage over an interval; saturation as a wait-queue length; errors as a count. Roughly 30 combinations for a typical server. vs the tools method, and this is the key contrast: USE iterates over resources, not tools. You build the complete list of questions first and then look for tools — so a question with no available tool becomes a recorded known-unknown, which is itself useful, rather than an invisible gap.
The USE order Errors first, saturation second, utilization third. Errors are usually objective rather than subjective, so they are quick to interpret and time-efficient to rule out. Saturation is next because any level of it can be an issue. vs starting with utilization, which is the metric that most needs interpretation — and the one whose two definitions can mislead. Note also that errors matter even when recoverable: operations that fail and retry, or a failed device in a redundant pool, degrade performance without being noticed.
USE on software and limits The same three questions applied to software resources and to imposed caps. A lock: utilization is time held, saturation is threads queued on it. A thread pool: utilization is time busy, saturation is requests waiting. Process, thread and file-descriptor capacity: usage, waiting on allocation, and allocation failures. Also applies to resource controls: a tenant's memory usage against its cap is capacity utilization, and limit-imposed allocation errors or swapping are saturation — even when the host system is not under memory pressure. The RED method For every service, check the request rate, errors and duration. Aimed at services, from the user's point of view. Draw the service architecture and make sure all three are monitored for each service. For duration, consider distribution statistics as well as the average. vs USE, and the two are complementary rather than competing: USE for machine health, RED for user health. RED's distinctive contribution is the request rate, which separates load from architecture: duration up with rate flat points at the service itself; both up means the problem may be one of load.
Workload characterization Four questions about the input rather than the resulting performance: who is causing the load, why, what are its characteristics, and how is it changing over time. Who: process, user, remote address. Why: code path or stack. What: rate, throughput, direction, type — with variance where appropriate. How: is there a daily pattern? vs analysis of the system, which may find nothing wrong: the architecture can be sound and the load simply more than it can handle. Check all four even when you are confident of the answers — the worked example is discovering that the clients are not the expected web servers but the entire internet, i.e. an attack. If the work cannot be eliminated, throttling it with resource controls is the fallback.
Drill-down analysis Three stages: monitoring (high-level statistics over time, and alerting), identification (narrow to particular resources or areas), analysis (root-cause and quantify). Monitoring is environment-wide and reveals long-term patterns that short command-line sessions miss. Analysis is where tracing and profiling tools come in, and where custom tooling gets written. Pair it with Five Whys: ask why, answer, and repeat about five times. The worked example runs from “queries are slow” through paging, memory growth and allocator behaviour to a memory fragmentation issue — a real case that ended in a fix to a system library. Persistent questioning is the mechanism; stopping at the first plausible answer is the failure.
Latency analysis Take the total time, split it into components, and keep subdividing the largest component until the cause is found and quantified. Is there a latency issue? On-CPU or off-CPU? Waiting on what — the file system? Disk I/O or lock contention? Queueing or servicing? Initialisation or data transfer? Each step divides the latency into two parts and analyses the larger one — a binary search of latency. Similar to drill-down in that it descends the stack, but the organising principle is different: drill-down narrows by area, latency analysis narrows by where the time is.
Event tracing Inspecting individual events rather than summaries: system calls, I/O, packets, queries. For each event look for the input (type, direction, size), the times (start, end, latency) and the result (error status, transfer size). vs summaries such as operations per second or average latency, where important detail is lost. The subtle payoff: a latency outlier may be caused by previous events rather than its own properties — the event at the tail of a queue is slow because of what was queued ahead of it, and only the traced sequence shows that.
Baseline statistics Collecting the full set of metrics while the system is under “normal” load, and recording them for later comparison. Collected at regular intervals and, importantly, before and after changes. It can be a script that runs the observability tools and captures their output. vs a monitoring dashboard, which covers only the metrics someone thought to monitor. Baselines answer “is this number normal for this server?” for the many command-line details that are not monitored. If no baseline exists, summary-since-boot figures from kernel counters are coarse but better than nothing. Beware the overhead of tracing tools in a baseline, so as not to perturb production.
Static performance tuning Checking the configured architecture, with the system at rest and no load applied. Four questions per component: does it make sense, does its configuration suit the intended workload, was it auto-configured well, and has it degraded after an error? An interface negotiated to a tenth of its speed; a failed disk in a redundant pool; an old version; a nearly-full file system; a record size mismatched to the workload; a debug mode left on; a server accidentally routing; a service reaching across data centres for something available locally. vs every other method here, which studies the dynamic performance of applied load. This is the one performed without load at all. These issues are easy to check for; the hard part is remembering to do it.
Micro-benchmarking Testing simple, artificial workloads, measuring the time for a large number of operations and calculating the average. System call time; file system reads from a cached file across sizes from one byte to a megabyte; network throughput between endpoints across socket buffer sizes. Note the second dimension in each — vary something. vs macro-benchmarking, which simulates a real-world workload and is complex to conduct and to understand. With fewer factors in play, micro-benchmarks are easier to reason about — and a network throughput test in particular can identify external network bottlenecks that would otherwise be difficult to spot, by examining the transport counters during a production workload. You may use a benchmark tool, or a load generator plus separate observability; either is fine, and cross-checking with a second tool is safest.

Cache tuning, in order

  1. Aim to cache as high in the stack as possible, closest to where the work is performed. This reduces the operational overhead of each hit, and that level usually has more metadata available to inform the retention policy.
  2. Check the cache is enabled and working at all.
  3. Check the hit/miss ratio and the miss rate.
  4. If the size is dynamic, check its current size.
  5. Tune the cache for the workload, using whatever tunables exist.
  6. Tune the workload for the cache — including removing unnecessary consumers, which frees space for the traffic you care about.

Two things to watch. Double caching: two caches both consuming main memory to hold the same data twice. And the size of the win at each level: improving a first-level processor cache may save nanoseconds, because a miss is served by the next level down; improving a last-level cache may avoid a far slower memory access and deliver a much larger overall gain.

Concept boundary — USE is not a checklist of tools, and finding something is not finishing

USE iterates over resources and only then asks what can measure them. The tools method iterates over tools. That difference is what converts an unanswerable question into a known-unknown instead of a blind spot.
And a system may have more than one problem. The first thing USE finds may be a problem but not the problem — each discovery should be investigated with other methods, and then you return to USE and continue iterating over the remaining resources.
Also worth stating: the negative result is valuable. Low utilization, no saturation, no errors, across the board, tells you it is probably not a resource problem — and narrowing the scope that fast is most of what the method buys you.

The numbers will lie to you

Scalability models, queueing theory, and the statistics that describe nothing that actually happened.

Tenth-grader version

  • Systems don't slow down gradually. They scale fine, then bend, then get worse — and there are only a few shapes that bend can take.
  • Queues have maths. A disk busy much past 60% of the time already answers about twice as slowly as an idle one.
  • An average is a single number standing in for a lot of numbers. Sometimes there's nothing in the middle for it to stand for.
  • If some requests take under a millisecond and the rest take about 7, the average can come out at 3.3 — and nothing that happened took 3.3.
  • So whenever someone says “the average is fine”, the right question is: what did the whole distribution look like?

The everyday version

A river with an average depth of six inches. You can walk it, obviously. Except the average is made of a wide shallow bank and a narrow deep channel, and the man in the proverb drowned in it. Now consider a single-lane toll booth. When it is idle you are through immediately; once it is busy rather more than 60% of the time you wait, on average, twice as long as when it was empty — not 60% longer, twice. And if someone tells you the booths ran at 40% utilization across the whole day, you still cannot say whether anyone queued, because the whole day contains a rush hour in which they were certainly at 100%.

The terms, each with its look-alike

Scalability profiles Five shapes you can identify visually, without any model, by plotting delivered performance against the thing you are scaling. Linear; contention for shared components used serially; coherence, where the tax of keeping data consistent outweighs the gain; a knee point where the profile changes; and a ceiling, a hard limit. vs a model. Visual identification is easy and effective, and worth doing first — the observed knee point can then be checked against a plausible cause, and confirmed experimentally by repeating the test on a system where that cause would move.
Amdahl's Law of Scalability C(N) = N / (1 + α(N − 1)), where C is relative capacity, N is the scaling dimension, and α is the degree of seriality (between 0 and 1). Fitted by collecting data across a range of N — by observation or by micro-benchmarking — then performing regression to find α, then plotting the model with the data. This is the contention profile: it accounts for the serial part of a workload that cannot run in parallel, and α is exactly how far the system deviates from linear.
Universal Scalability Law C(N) = N / (1 + α(N − 1) + βN(N − 1)), adding β, the coherency parameter. This is the coherence profile, and it includes the effects of contention as well. vs Amdahl, which it contains: when β = 0 it becomes Amdahl's Law. The practical value of fitting a model at all is that a deviation between model and data is informative — either your understanding of the system is wrong, or the system's real scalability is.
Little's Law L = λW: the average number of requests in a system equals the average arrival rate multiplied by the average time each spends in it. Applied to a queue: L is the number waiting, W is the average wait. One of the foundations queueing theory is built on, alongside probability distributions, stochastic processes and Erlang's C formula. Queueing systems let you answer questions like: what will mean response time be if load doubles, or if we add a processor?
Kendall's notation A/S/m: the arrival process, the service time distribution, and the number of service centres. M/M/1 Markovian arrivals and service times, one server. M/M/c the same, multi-server. M/G/1 Markovian arrivals, general service times — commonly applied to rotational disks. M/D/1 Markovian arrivals, deterministic (fixed) service times. An extended form adds buffers, population size and service discipline. In queueing theory the service centres are usually called servers, which is not the same word you use for a machine.
M/D/1 past 60% r = s(2 − ρ) / 2(1 − ρ), where r is response time, s is service time and ρ is utilization. Past 60% utilization the mean response time has doubled. By 80% it has tripled. An idle rotational disk might serve I/O in about 1 ms; under load that can approach 10 ms. Why disk utilization is a problem well before 100%: it is a queueing system whose requests typically cannot be interrupted and must wait their turn. CPUs differ — higher-priority work can preempt. And this model is the best case: variation in service time drives the mean higher, and the 90th and 99th percentiles degrade much faster than the mean beyond 60%.
Resource limit analysis Measure the request rate, measure resource usage, express requests in terms of resources used, and extrapolate to the known limit. 1,000 requests/s with 16 CPUs at 40% gives 16 × 40% / 1,000 = 0.64% CPU per request, so 100% × 16 / 0.64 = 2,500 requests/s at saturation. That is a rough best-case estimate: some other factor may bind first. It also used a single data point, and monitoring over time gives several at different rates, which improves the extrapolation. Then ask the separate question — is 2,500/s enough? A site averaging one request a second may take most of its 100,000 daily hits in the seconds after new content appears.
Factor analysis Testing a limited set of configurations rather than all of them: measure at maximum, change one factor at a time, attribute a percentage drop and a cost saving to each, then compose the cheapest configuration that still meets the requirement — and retest it. Eight binary factors would need 256 tests exhaustively; this approach needs about ten. The retest at the end is not optional: components can behave differently in combination than their individual measurements predict. In the worked example, dropping to two processors and one network card gives 2 × (1 − 0.30) × (1 − 0.25) ≈ 1.04 GB/s against a 1 GB/s requirement — a margin thin enough that confirming it matters.
Quantifying gains Two ways. Observation: choose a reliable metric, estimate the gain from resolving the issue. Experiment: apply the fix and compare before with after. Observed: a request takes 10 ms, of which 9 ms is disk I/O; caching in memory at around 10 μs gives 10 ms → 1.01 ms, roughly a 9× gain. Experimental: 10 ms → 2 ms after raising concurrency, a 5× gain. Latency suits this because it can be compared directly across components. One caution: make sure it is measured as a synchronous part of the request — background work such as a write flush does not directly affect the request's time. Use observation rather than experiment when the fix is expensive to attempt in production.
Which mean The arithmetic mean is the usual one. The geometric mean is the nth root of the product; the harmonic mean is the count divided by the sum of reciprocals. Geometric suits improvements that compound multiplicatively — several layers of a stack each improved, all acting on the same packet. Harmonic suits rates: 800 MB, the first 100 at 50 MB/s and the rest at 10 MB/s, gives 800/(100/50 + 700/10) = 11.1 MB/s. vs reaching for the arithmetic mean by default, which for an average of rates is simply the wrong operation. Also note the decayed average, still over an interval but weighting recent time more heavily, which dampens short-term fluctuation — the familiar load averages work this way.
Averages over time Nearly every performance metric is an average over some interval, and the interval is part of the number. A CPU is never “at 50% utilization”; it was utilized during 50% of an interval — a second, a minute, an hour. The failure this causes: a monitoring tool reporting five-minute averages never showed above 80%, while the real cause was CPU saturation during periods when utilization hit 100% for seconds at a time. Always check the interval before believing an average is reassuring.
Coefficient of variation The ratio of the standard deviation to the mean, expressed as a single number. A standard deviation of 50 tells you almost nothing on its own; a standard deviation of 50 with a mean of 200 tells you a great deal — a CoV of 25%. Lower means less variance. A related single-figure expression is the z value, how many standard deviations a particular value sits from the mean.
Multimodal distribution Two or more clusters. Means, standard deviations and percentiles are intended for normal-like or unimodal data. Disk I/O latency for a mixed workload: one mode under 1 ms (on-disk cache hits) and one peaking near 7 ms (misses, random reads). The mean is 3.3 ms. That 3.3 ms is not an index of central tendency — it is almost the opposite, sitting in the gap between the two things that actually happen. System performance is frequently bimodal: a fast code path and a slow one, or cache hits and cache misses. Every time you see an average latency, ask what the distribution is.
Outliers A very small number of extreme values that do not fit the distribution, whether it is unimodal or not. Occasional disk I/O taking over 1,000 ms when the majority are between 0 and 10 ms. Network latency outliers from timer-based retransmits are another. They cause serious problems and are hard to see in most metric types other than a maximum. For a normal distribution their presence shifts the mean a little but not the median — so a mean and median that disagree is a signal. The standard deviation and the 99th percentile have a better chance of catching them, but that still depends on how often they occur.

The pictures that answer “what is the distribution?”

  • Line chart. The familiar one: a metric against time. Adding lines for the median, standard deviation and percentiles to the same axes turns it from a summary into something informative — in the worked example the mean read latency sits around 4 ms, higher than expected, and the extra lines show why: the median is around 1 ms, where the latency was expected to be, and 1% of the I/O is over 20 ms.
  • Scatter plot. Every event drawn as a point — completion time against latency. It shows all the data, which is how the outliers at 10, 20 and over 50 ms become visible at all. Its limits are resolution, where dense points overlap and merge into a wall of paint, and the volume of data that must be collected and processed.
  • Heat map. Quantise both axes into buckets and colour each block by how many events fall in it. This solves both scatter-plot problems at once: it can show one system or thousands the same way, and the bulk patterns emerge instead of drowning. High-latency outliers appear as isolated blocks high in the map, and a bimodal distribution is immediately legible.
  • Timeline chart. Activities as bars on a timeline — used for request waterfalls in browsers and for threads or CPUs on servers. With explicit dependency arrows it becomes a Gantt chart.
  • Surface plot. Three dimensions rendered as a surface, best when the third value changes smoothly. Per-CPU utilization across a fleet: each server's CPUs as rows, per-second measurements as columns, height and colour set by the value. A server pinned at 100% appears as a plateau; a single CPU running constantly at a few percent appears as a faint line.

Monitoring is what gives a number its context

Recording metrics over time lets the past be compared with the present, which is how you know what “normal” was. Expect cycles: hourly (reporting and monitoring tasks, often on five- or ten-minute cycles too), daily (work hours, stretched across time zones; plus nightly log rotation and backups), weekly (workdays against weekends), quarterly (financial reporting), and yearly (school terms and holidays). Irregular increases come from content releases and sales events; irregular decreases come from power and internet outages, and from everybody watching a sports final instead of using your product.

Concept boundary — a summary statistic is a claim about the shape of the data

A mean is an index of central tendency — but only if the data has a centre. For a bimodal distribution it can land precisely where nothing happens, which makes it not merely imprecise but actively misleading.
Percentiles are better and not sufficient. They quantify the slow tail, which is why they appear in service-level agreements, and the 50th — the median — shows where the bulk of the data sits. But they are still summaries of a distribution they assume is well behaved.
The only reliable move is to look at the full distribution: a histogram, a scatter plot, a heat map. Everything else is a claim you have not checked.

Answer key

Part 1 · Q1 — the array that is “maxed out”

(a) The dashboard is almost certainly reporting time-based utilization — U = B/T, the fraction of the interval the resource was busy, which a disk tool would more honestly call percent busy. That is not the same as capacity-based utilization, the fraction of deliverable throughput in use. Storage arrays frequently run at 100% precisely because some disk is busy the whole time while the array as a whole has plenty of idle disks and can accept more work. The everyday example is a lift: it may be moving between floors 100% of the time and still have room for passengers. 100% busy does not mean 100% capacity.

(b) The morning window can be real because an average conceals its interval. Five-minute averages mask short periods at 100%, and a short burst of high utilization is enough to cause saturation and a queue — the toll-plaza case, where a 40% daily average tells you nothing about whether anyone queued during rush hour. The metric that settles it is saturation, expressed as a wait-queue length or as time spent waiting, because unlike utilization it has no ambiguous form: any non-zero value is work that arrived and could not be serviced, which is latency someone experienced.

(c) Collect the queue length (and errors) for the array, at a much shorter interval than five minutes, over the 09:15–09:40 window — that combination distinguishes “busy but coping” from “out of capacity”, and it is the pair the utilization figure cannot separate. The missing queue-length metric is a known-unknown: you know the metric exists and you have not observed it. Recording it as such is a result rather than a gap — it is precisely the kind of thing a tool-first approach would have left as an invisible blind spot. Credit for noting that a purchase justified by a number that cannot distinguish the two cases is unjustified.

Part 1 · Q2 — the cache, the forum tunable and the redundant work

(a) Two independent reasons. First, the cache was almost certainly cold for most of a ten-minute run: caches start cold and warm over time, and when the cache is large or the next tier slow, warming takes far longer than people expect — the worked case is a 128 GB memory cache filling at 16 MB/s and taking over two hours. A benchmark shorter than the warm-up measures the wrong thing. Second, the hit ratio's effect is strongly non-linear, so a partially warm cache sitting at a low ratio produces very little of the benefit a hot cache would: the difference between 98% and 99% is far larger than between 10% and 11%. Also creditable: the right figure to check is the miss rate, not the ratio, since the misses are what cost time.

(b) It is a point-in-time recommendation. A tunable found elsewhere may be inappropriate for this system and workload, may have been appropriate once and not now, or may be a temporary workaround for a bug already fixed in a later version — the comparison drawn is with taking someone else's prescription medicine. What it is good for is discovering which parameters exist and have needed changing; your job then becomes deciding whether and how they should be tuned here. Note also that you may still miss an important parameter simply because nobody else has needed to change it. If adopted, it should be stored in version control with the reason and date.

(c) Ranking: the redundant decoding first, then the cache, then the storage tunable. The principle is that tuning is most effective closest to where the work is performed — at the application level you can eliminate work entirely, for gains of the order of 20×, whereas tuning down at the storage device happens after the higher-level stack has already executed and yields percentages, of the order of 20%. Re-reading and re-decoding the same image is unnecessary work, and eliminating unnecessary work is the largest win available. The caveat: the most effective level to tune is not necessarily the most effective level from which to observe — the redundant decoding may be easiest to see from operating system tools showing file system and disk I/O, or time on-CPU, rather than from inside the application.

Part 2 · Q1 — the checkout incident

(a) The first engineer is using the streetlight anti-method — opening the tools they know and looking for something odd — followed by the blame-someone-else anti-method, redirecting to a team they are not on. The second is using the random change anti-method. What all three share, and the identifying feature to name, is that the hypothesis arrived before the data, or with no data behind it at all. Note also the first engineer's specific failure: elevated disk activity may be an issue, but nothing in the approach quantified it, so there is no basis for believing it is the issue.

(b) The problem statement should have come first, since it is the method to attempt before any other on a new issue. Its six questions: what makes you think there is a performance problem; has this system ever performed well; what changed recently, in software, hardware or load; can the problem be expressed in terms of latency or runtime; does it affect other people or applications; and what is the environment, with software, hardware, versions and configuration. It costs almost nothing because it requires no tools, no access and no metrics — issues have been resolved this way over the phone without logging in to any server. In this scenario the third question alone would likely have identified a recent change, and nobody has yet asked it.

(c) Two problems. First, changing three values at once destroys attribution: they cannot know which change mattered, and the discipline is to reverse a change that does not work before forming the next hypothesis, precisely because changing multiple factors at once makes it impossible to identify which one was responsible. Second, the one that appears later: they have left behind tuning nobody understood in the first place. If it happens to work around a bug in the database or operating system that is later fixed, the tuning remains, no longer makes sense, and nobody will dare remove it. Also creditable: a poorly understood change can cause a worse problem at peak production load, requiring an emergency rollback.

Part 2 · Q2 — the 30-entry checklist

(a) The checklist is genuinely valuable: an ad hoc checklist can provide the most value in the shortest time frame, and it is an effective way to ensure a whole team knows how to check for common issues, written clearly and prescriptively. Its two structural limitations: every entry is a point-in-time recommendation that needs frequent refreshing to stay current — which explains the tunable that made things worse — and checklists skew toward issues with known, easily documented fixes, typically tunable settings, rather than custom fixes to source code or the environment. That explains the two silent failures.

(b) Adding tools deepens the tools method, which relies exclusively on the tools you have or know and therefore gives an incomplete view of the system. The specific blind spot, and the reason it is dangerous rather than merely limited, is that the user is unaware that the view is incomplete — and may remain unaware. Issues requiring custom tooling may never be identified. It also scales badly: with many tools of overlapping functionality, time goes into evaluating which to use rather than into the investigation.

(c) Put the problem statement first on every issue, then a hypothesis-driven loop — the scientific method (question, hypothesis, prediction, test, analysis) or the tighter diagnosis cycle (hypothesis → instrumentation → data → hypothesis), moving to data quickly so bad theories are discarded early. Where a resource sweep is wanted, prefer the USE method, because it iterates over resources rather than tools: you build the complete list of questions first and only then look for something that can answer them. And the answer to the last part: a question you cannot answer should be recorded as a known-unknown. Knowing which questions are unanswered is extremely useful, and it is exactly the information a tool-first approach conceals.

Section 9 continued

Part 3 · Q1 — the payments service that slowed over three weeks

(a) Duration has risen while the request rate is unchanged, which points at the architecture — the service itself — rather than at load. That is the distinction the request rate buys you: if both rate and duration had risen, the problem might be the load applied, and the investigation would go to workload characterization. Here it rules that out early, before any deep analysis.

(b) Run the USE method: for every resource, check utilization, saturation and errors. The order is errors first, because they are usually objective rather than subjective and so are quick to interpret and time-efficient to rule out; saturation second, because it is quicker to interpret than utilization and any level of it can be an issue; utilization last. For a capped container, apply the same three questions to the resource controls rather than only to the physical hardware: memory capacity utilization is the tenant's usage against its cap, and memory capacity saturation shows up as limit-imposed allocation errors or swapping for that tenant even when the host system is not experiencing memory pressure. That is exactly why the calm host dashboards are not reassuring — they are measuring the wrong boundary.

(c) Static performance tuning: check the configured architecture with no load applied, asking of each component whether it makes sense, whether its configuration suits the intended workload, whether it was auto-configured into the best state, and whether it has degraded after an error. Eleven months without review is exactly the situation it exists for — a negotiated-down network interface, a failed disk in a redundant pool, an old version, a nearly-full file system, a debug mode accidentally left enabled. These are easy to check for; the hard part is remembering to do it. Also creditable: baseline statistics, or failing that summary-since-boot values, to establish whether current figures are normal for this server.

Part 3 · Q2 — the reporting database and the hanging queries

(a) Workload characterization, which focuses on the input to the system rather than the resulting performance. Its four questions: who is causing the load (process, user, remote address), why is it being called (code path, stack trace), what are its characteristics (rate, throughput, direction, type — with variance where appropriate), and how is it changing over time (is there a daily pattern). They should be asked even when the team is confident because you may be surprised: the worked example is a team who checked the client addresses of a database they expected to be used by their web servers, and discovered the entire internet was sending load — a denial-of-service attack. A weekday-morning pattern is itself a strong hint that the fourth question matters here.

(b) Event tracing. Summaries such as an average latency destroy exactly the detail that matters when a small number of operations behave very differently from the rest; tracing inspects events individually, recording for each the input (type, direction, size), the times (start, end, latency) and the result (error status, transfer size). It also surfaces something no summary can: a latency outlier may be caused by preceding events rather than by its own properties — an event at the tail of a queue is slow because of what was queued ahead of it. Also creditable: the distribution is probably multimodal, so the average is not an index of central tendency, and a histogram or heat map would show the modes.

(c) Before adding capacity you must establish whether this is a problem of load or of architecture — adding instances is the correct response to load, and does nothing for a single-threaded bottleneck or lock contention. The ordering principle is the performance mantras, most effective first: don't do it (eliminate unnecessary work); do it but don't do it again (caching); do it less (reduce refresh or polling frequency); do it later (write-back); do it when they're not looking (schedule off-peak); do it concurrently; and only last, do it more cheaply — buy faster hardware. Doubling the instances is the last item on that list, being proposed first. For a weekday-morning peak, “do it when they're not looking” is a particularly live option.

Part 4 · Q1 — the storage that is “healthy”

(a) Two independent reasons. First, a multimodal distribution: system performance is frequently bimodal — a fast path and a slow one, or on-disk cache hits and misses — and the mean then falls in the gap between the modes. In the worked case, modes under 1 ms and near 7 ms give a mean of 3.3 ms, which is not an index of central tendency; it is almost the opposite, and describes nothing that actually happened. Second, outliers: a very small number of extreme values, such as occasional disk I/O over 1,000 ms among a majority between 0 and 10 ms. These barely move the mean, do not move the median at all, and are exactly what a user experiences as a multi-second stall. Whether the 99th percentile catches them depends on how frequent they are; a maximum may be the only summary that shows them.

(b) 55% utilization is not the reassurance they think. Modelling the disk as M/D/1 gives r = s(2 − ρ)/2(1 − ρ), and beyond 60% utilization the mean response time has doubled; by 80% it has tripled. So they are already close to the point where queueing dominates, and this is the best case — variation in service time drives the mean higher still, and the 90th and 99th percentiles degrade much faster than the mean past 60%. It applies more forcefully to disks than to CPUs because a disk is a queueing system whose requests typically cannot be interrupted and must wait their turn, whereas on a CPU higher-priority work can preempt. Add that the 55% is itself a five-minute average, which hides shorter periods at much higher utilization.

(c) Ask for the full distribution and a shorter interval rather than three summary numbers — and specifically for saturation (queue length) alongside utilization. The visualization is a heat map: it quantises both axes into buckets and colours by event count, which solves the two problems of a scatter plot at this scale — the visual density limit, where dense points merge into a wall of paint, and the volume of data that must be collected and processed for every event. It shows one system or thousands the same way, makes a bimodal distribution immediately legible, and leaves high-latency outliers visible as isolated blocks high in the map.

Part 4 · Q2 — the campaign and the scaling claim

(a) CPU per request = total CPU% ÷ requests = 24 × 45% ÷ 2,000 = 1,080 ÷ 2,000 = 0.54% CPU per request. Maximum requests/s = 100% × 24 CPUs ÷ 0.54 = 2,400 ÷ 0.54 ≈ 4,440 requests/s. Two reasons it is optimistic: it is a rough best-case estimate — some other limiting factor may be encountered before requests reach that rate — and it rests on a single data point, one throughput figure against one utilization figure, where monitoring over time would give several pairs and a much better extrapolation. Also creditable: long before 100% the queueing effects of the previous question apply; and a tripling of traffic to 6,000 requests/s exceeds the estimate by a wide margin anyway.

(b) Throughput rising to a peak and then falling as instances are added is the coherence profile — the tax of maintaining coherency, including propagating changes, has begun to outweigh the benefit of scaling. Amdahl's Law of Scalability models contention only, C(N) = N/(1 + α(N−1)), which flattens but does not decline; you need the Universal Scalability Law, C(N) = N/(1 + α(N−1) + βN(N−1)), whose β term is coherency delay — and note that with β = 0 the USL becomes Amdahl. The implication for the proposal is direct and uncomfortable: the system does not scale linearly, and past the peak adding instances reduces throughput, so doubling the count may deliver less capacity than they have now, not twice as much.

(c) Measure across a range of N — by observation over time or experimentally with micro-benchmarking or a load generator — and fit the model by regression to obtain α and β, then plot the data against the model, because a deviation is itself informative: either the model is wrong or your understanding of the system's real scalability is. Also collect multiple throughput-and-utilization pairs to firm up the resource-limit extrapolation. The method for composing the cheapest configuration that still meets the requirement is factor analysis: test at maximum, change one factor at a time and attribute a percentage drop and a cost saving to each, then choose the combination that meets the target — eight binary factors need about ten tests rather than 256 — and retest the chosen configuration, since components can behave differently together than their individual measurements predict.

Two marks, both quick. Completeness: did you name the method, and — for this competency especially — did you say which situation it is the answer to?
