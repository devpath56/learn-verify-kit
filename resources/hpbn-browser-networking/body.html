Section 1

Learning goal & roadmap

Learning goal — what you should be able to do when you close this

Given a real performance question (“the page is slow, should we buy more bandwidth?”, “why did sharding make mobile worse?”, “should this feature use polling, SSE or a socket?”, “why does one big message stall all the others?”), you can:

  1. Name the delay — propagation, transmission, processing or queuing — and say which of them the proposed fix actually touches.
  2. Count the round trips before and after. Almost every argument in this subject is settled by that count.
  3. Prescribe the transport — plain HTTP, SSE, or WebSocket — and name the near-miss you rejected and why.
  4. State the price. Every technique here trades round trips against sockets, cache granularity, memory, battery, or the browser services you stop receiving.

The core model in one paragraph

Two numbers decide the performance of any network transfer: bandwidth, the maximum throughput of the path, and latency, the time for a packet to get from one end to the other. They are not symmetrical problems. Bandwidth is an engineering and money problem — lay more fibre, light more wavelengths — and it has been improving steadily. Latency is bounded by the speed of light, and we are already within a small constant factor of it, so the most a better cable can buy is a modest improvement. Everything else in this booklet follows from that asymmetry. A protocol that spends an extra round trip per resource is spending the one resource that cannot be manufactured, which is why HTTP/1.x forces a pile of workarounds — reuse the connection, open six of them, shard the domain, bundle the files — and why, when the server needs to send you something, the interesting question is whether you can avoid paying a fresh round trip to ask for it at all. Parts 3 and 4 are the two answers to that: one cheap and text-only, one that gives you the whole socket and charges you every service the browser used to provide.

Tenth-grader version — the whole thing at once

  • Two things decide how fast the network feels: how wide the pipe is, and how long it takes one thing to get to the other end.
  • You can widen the pipe with money. You cannot make light faster — so distance is a hard floor.
  • So the whole game is: ask fewer times, and don't wait for one answer before asking the next question.
  • Old HTTP can only carry one answer at a time down a connection, so browsers open several and developers glue files together to ask less often.
  • And if the server wants to tell you something, it either keeps one connection open and talks down it, or you open a proper two-way pipe — which is fast, but the browser then stops helping you with caching and compression.

Latency is the ceiling

Four delays, one of which you can buy your way out of, and one of which is physics.

Tenth-grader version

  • “Fast internet” is really two different things: how much you can send at once, and how long one thing takes to arrive.
  • A wide pipe doesn't help if you have to wait for a reply before you can send the next thing. That waiting is latency.
  • Some of the wait is because the signal has to physically travel. Light in glass does about 200,000 km per second, and you cannot argue with that.
  • Some of it is because pushing bits onto a slow link takes time — that part does get better with a faster connection.
  • And some of it is your packet sitting in a queue inside a router, which is the one nobody expects.

The everyday version

Posting a parcel. Propagation delay is how long the van takes to drive the distance — set by geography and the speed of the van, and the only way to shorten it is to move the warehouse closer. Transmission delay is how long it takes to load the parcels onto the van at the depot — a bigger loading bay genuinely fixes that, and it has nothing to do with how far the van is going. Processing delay is the few seconds a sorting office spends reading each label. Queuing delay is your parcel sitting in a full sorting office because more arrived than could be handled. Buying a bigger loading bay does nothing for the drive, and the drive is usually what you are waiting for.

The terms, each with its look-alike

TermPlain definitionConcrete exampleBoundary — what it gets confused with
Latency The time from the source sending a packet to the destination receiving it. New York to London is 5,585 km: 28 ms one way in fibre, 56 ms for the round trip. vs bandwidth, the maximum throughput of the path. They are different quantities with different economics, and conflating them is the single most common mistake in this subject. For most websites, latency is the bottleneck.
Propagation delay Time for a message to travel from sender to receiver: distance ÷ signal speed. Light in a vacuum is 299,792,458 m/s. In optical fibre the rule of thumb is 200,000,000 m/s, a refractive index of about 1.5. vs transmission delay. Propagation is a function of distance and nothing else; transmission is a function of link rate and nothing else. This is the delay you cannot buy your way out of, and the only lever on it is shorter distance — which is what a content delivery network is for.
Transmission delay Time to push all the packet's bits onto the link: packet length ÷ data rate. A 10 Mb file takes 10 seconds to put on the wire over a 1 Mbps link and 0.1 seconds over a 100 Mbps link. vs propagation again: this one has nothing to do with the distance between client and server. It is also the delay that more bandwidth genuinely fixes, which is why bandwidth upgrades help large transfers and barely help page loads.
Processing delay Time for a router to examine the packet header, check for bit-level errors, and determine the outgoing route. Much of this is now done in hardware, so the delays are very small — but they exist, and they are incurred at every hop. vs queuing delay, which is time spent waiting to be processed rather than time spent being processed. More intermediate routers raise processing and transmission delay for each packet; heavier traffic raises queuing delay.
Queuing delay Time an incoming packet waits in a router's buffer before it can be processed. If packets arrive faster than the router can handle, they are buffered — and the wait is queuing delay. Bufferbloat is this delay gone wrong: routers shipped with very large incoming buffers on the assumption that dropping packets should be avoided at all costs. That breaks TCP's congestion avoidance mechanisms and introduces high and variable latency. Active queue management — the CoDel algorithm — was proposed to address it.
Last-mile latency The delay in the first few hops between you and your provider's core network. One regulator's peak-hour measurements: 18 ms average for fibre-to-the-home, 26 ms for cable, 44 ms for DSL — just to reach the nearest measuring node. vs the intuition that crossing oceans is the expensive part. It is often the last few miles, not the continents, where significant latency appears. When choosing a provider for browsing speed, low latency is worth optimising for.
Bandwidth at the edge Available throughput is set by the lowest-capacity link anywhere between client and server. Core fibre links carry hundreds of terabits per second — over 400 wavelengths multiplexed at up to 171 Gbit/s each, more than 70 Tbit/s per fibre. The edge is nothing like that. vs assuming a fast link to your provider guarantees end-to-end performance. It does not: any intermediate node can be congested from demand, hardware failure or attack. High variability of both throughput and latency is an inherent property of these networks.
Great-circle path The shortest route between two points on the globe — the best case for propagation. New York to Sydney is 15,993 km, giving a 160 ms round trip in fibre along the great circle. vs the real number. No such cable exists, so the packet takes a much longer route, and each extra hop adds routing, processing, queuing and transmission delay. The actual round trip is in the 200–300 ms range.

Why the two problems have different futures

  • Bandwidth is solvable. Add more fibres to a link, deploy more links along congested routes, or improve the multiplexing to push more data through the fibre you already have. Between 2007 and 2011, more than half the added capacity on trans-Pacific cables came from better equipment at both ends of existing fibre, not new cable — and as of 2011 only about 20% of deployed subsea capacity was in use. Every medium eventually reaches diminishing returns, but there is no fundamental reason throughput cannot keep increasing.
  • Latency is not. Better materials with a lower refractive index and faster routers would help a little, but we are already within roughly 1.5× of the speed of light, so the most that strategy can deliver is a modest ~30% improvement. The alternative is to make the distance shorter, which means laying cable along the great circle — constrained by terrain, politics and cost.
  • So you design around it. Reduce round trips, move the data closer to the client, and hide what remains behind caching and pre-fetching. Those are the only three moves, and everything in parts 2 to 4 is one of them.

The numbers that make it a human problem

Most people reliably report perceptible lag once a delay over 100–200 ms is introduced. Past 300 ms the interaction is described as sluggish. At 1,000 ms many users have already performed a mental context switch — a daydream, or the next task. Set that against a 160 ms round trip to Sydney under ideal conditions and the budget becomes obvious: applications need to respond within hundreds of milliseconds, and the network does not leave much room for error.

Concept boundary — “faster internet” is not one thing

More bandwidth reduces transmission delay. It helps most when you are moving a lot of bytes at once — a video stream, a large download.
It does not touch propagation delay, which is set by distance, so it barely helps a page that makes many small requests and waits for each answer. That page is spending round trips, not bytes.
The test: if you doubled the link speed, would the thing you are waiting for finish in half the time? If it wouldn't, you have a latency problem, and no amount of capacity will fix it.

What HTTP/1.x makes you do

One missing feature — multiplexing — and the pile of workarounds it generates.

Tenth-grader version

  • Setting up a connection costs a round trip before you've asked for anything. Reusing one connection instead of opening a new one for every file is the single biggest easy win.
  • You'd think you could fire off all your questions at once. You can — but the answers must come back in order, so one slow answer holds up every answer behind it.
  • Because of that, browsers just open six connections at once and hope.
  • Developers then split their files across several hostnames to get more than six, and glue small files together to ask fewer times.
  • None of that is clever. It is all patching around one thing the protocol cannot do: send two answers down one connection at the same time.

The everyday version

A serving hatch with one window. You can queue up and order one dish at a time, waiting at the hatch after each order — that is a connection without reuse, and you walk back and forth for every dish. Keepalive means you stay at the hatch. Pipelining means you shout all your orders at once, which is better — except the hatch is only wide enough to pass one plate at a time and they must come out in the order you asked. So if your first dish takes twenty minutes, the salad that was ready in one minute sits on the counter behind it. That is head-of-line blocking, and the restaurant's answer is not a wider hatch: it is opening six hatches.

The terms, each with its look-alike

TermPlain definitionConcrete exampleBoundary — what it gets confused with
Keepalive (persistent connection) Reusing one TCP connection for several HTTP requests. Two names, one feature. Without it, every request costs two round trips — one for the handshake, one for request and response. With it, only the first does. The saving for N requests is (N−1) × RTT, and the average page is now 90-plus resources, so the potential saving is measured in seconds. Enabled by default in HTTP 1.1. Watch your libraries: many default to non-keepalive because it gives a simpler API.
Pipelining Dispatching requests without waiting for each response, moving the first-in-first-out queue from the client to the server. Eliminates the idle round trip between responses, and lets the server process requests in parallel. vs multiplexing, which HTTP 1.x does not have. Pipelining reorders when requests are sent; it does not allow response data to be interleaved. That distinction is the whole of the next row.
Head-of-line blocking HTTP 1.x requires strict serialisation of responses: each must be returned in full before the next one's bytes can be sent. Client requests HTML (40 ms) and CSS (20 ms) together. The server finishes the CSS first — and must buffer it until the HTML is fully sent. vs the TCP version of the same phrase, where a lost packet blocks every packet with a higher sequence number until it is retransmitted. Same name, different layer. The consequences here: underused links, server buffering costs, and unpredictable client latency.
Six connections per host The browser's workaround for the absence of multiplexing: open several TCP sessions to the same origin. Six parallel requests, six parallel server-side processes, and the initial congestion window effectively multiplied by six. vs a solution. It buys parallelism at the cost of sockets, memory and CPU on client, server and every intermediary, plus streams competing for the same bandwidth. The number is a safe middle ground, not a calculation.
Self-inflicted denial of service The connection limit protects the server — and can block your own application. Open six parallel downloads to one host and issue a seventh: it hangs until one completes. The trap: a WebSocket, an SSE stream or a hanging request each occupies a full TCP stream even while idle, because there is no multiplexing. Three long-lived connections leave you three ordinary requests.
Domain sharding Splitting resources across several hostnames so the browser's per-host limit applies several times. Shards are often CNAME records pointing at the same server: limits are enforced per hostname, not per IP address. vs a free win. Each new hostname costs a DNS lookup, more sockets on both sides, and manual management of where resources live. Overused, it produces many connections that never escape TCP slow-start. There is no formula for the right number — it depends on the page and on each client's bandwidth and latency.
Protocol overhead The HTTP metadata carried on every request, always in plain text, uncompressed. 500–800 bytes per browser-initiated request before cookies. A 15-character JSON body wrapped in 352 bytes of headers is 96% overhead. vs the payload, which is often the smaller half. Headers are plain text for backwards compatibility; the standard sets no size limit, though many servers and proxies enforce 8 KB or 16 KB. Cookies are the usual largest offender and the usual forgotten one.
Concatenation · spriting Combining several scripts or stylesheets into one resource; combining several images into one composite. The byte delivery is the same as if pipelining worked — you have moved the pipelining logic up into your application. vs free. All the bundled resources share one cache key, so a one-line change invalidates the lot; the bundle may contain code this page never needs; a sprite must be decoded and held in memory whole; and neither CSS nor JavaScript executes until the entire file has arrived.
Resource inlining Embedding a resource in the document — script and style blocks, or the data: URI scheme for anything else. Rule of thumb: consider it for resources under 1–2 KB, below which the HTTP overhead often exceeds the resource. vs bundling. An inlined resource is part of the page, so it cannot be cached separately by the browser, a CDN or any proxy — inline it on ten pages and it ships ten times. Non-text assets need base64, which costs 33% byte expansion.

The arithmetic of a round trip saved

  • The minimum cost of one HTTP request over a new TCP connection is two network round trips: one for the handshake, one for the request-response cycle. That is a fixed cost on every non-persistent session, on top of whatever the server takes to produce the answer.
  • The faster your server, the more the fixed cost dominates. Halving your server time does nothing to the two round trips.
  • Worked example over a 28 ms one-way link, fetching an HTML document (40 ms of server time) and a CSS file (20 ms): 284 ms over two separate connections; 228 ms with keepalive, which removes one handshake; 172 ms with pipelining, which removes an idle round trip between the two responses. That is a 40% reduction from protocol choices alone — and the saving is not fixed. The higher the latency and the more requests, the larger it gets.
  • One vendor reported a 3× improvement for users on slower networks after making better use of existing connections through keepalive and pipelining in a desktop client.

Why pipelining failed, precisely

In theory the server could process pipelined requests in parallel and save another 20 ms. In practice, strict response serialisation means it cannot deliver them out of order, and that produces a list of problems the standard gives no guidance on:

  • A single slow response blocks every request behind it.
  • Servers processing in parallel must buffer the completed responses — and a very large response can exhaust server resources, which is an attack vector.
  • A failed response may terminate the connection, forcing the client to re-request everything after it, which can duplicate processing.
  • Detecting whether pipelining is safe is hard when intermediaries may be present: some abort the connection, others serialise everything.

Consequently browsers ship with it disabled or hidden behind an advanced setting. It remains genuinely useful when you control both ends — your client and your server, your retries and your idempotency, tunnelled over TLS so intermediaries cannot interfere.

Concept boundary — these are workarounds, not best practices

Two recommendations are evergreen and will be true under any protocol: eliminate unnecessary network latency, and minimise the bytes transferred. Fewer DNS lookups, fewer requests, a CDN, cache headers, compression, no redirects — all of these follow from those two.
Sharding, bundling, spriting and inlining are different. They exist to paper over one missing protocol feature. They are necessary today and you should know them cold, but they are not virtues, and each one buys its round trips with cache granularity, memory or complexity.
The tell: if a technique would become pointless the moment the protocol could interleave responses, it is a workaround.

Push, cheaply

Server-Sent Events: one HTTP response that never ends, and how much the browser does for you.

Tenth-grader version

  • Normally you ask and the server answers. If you want to know the moment something changes, asking over and over is wasteful and always slightly late.
  • Instead the server answers once and simply never finishes: it keeps the connection open and writes new lines as things happen.
  • The format is almost embarrassingly simple — lines beginning data:, separated by blank lines.
  • The browser does the fiddly parts: it splits the messages, tells your code about each one, and if the connection drops it reconnects on its own and says which message it got last.
  • Two catches: the server can talk, but you cannot talk back on it; and it is text only.

The everyday version

A live radio commentary versus phoning the ground every thirty seconds to ask the score. Phoning back repeatedly costs a call each time and you are always up to half a minute behind — that is polling. The commentary is one call that stays open, and you hear each goal the moment it happens. If the line drops, a good service dials back automatically and says “you last heard the 41st minute”, so nothing is missed. What you cannot do is talk back down the commentary line, and you cannot receive a photograph on it.

What the wire actually looks like

The client sends an ordinary request with Accept: text/event-stream. The server replies 200 with Content-Type: text/event-stream, a keepalive connection and chunked transfer encoding — and then simply keeps writing. Everything after that is text:

  • retry: 15000 sets the client's reconnect delay to 15 seconds.
  • A line of data: followed by a blank line is a complete message with no type — it triggers the generic message callback.
  • Adding event: foo makes it fire as a custom DOM event of type foo instead.
  • Adding id: 42 makes the browser remember it, and send it back on the next reconnect.
  • Two adjacent data: lines are one message with a newline in it, not two messages.

That is the entire protocol. It is trivial to implement on a server, and the reason it is worth learning as a format rather than an API is that the simplicity of the format is exactly what lets the browser take the work off you.

Two deployment realities worth knowing before you ship it

Intermediaries. Proxies and firewalls that are not stream-aware may buffer the event stream, which turns into increased latency or an outright broken connection. The reliable answer is to deliver the stream over TLS, so nothing in the middle is in a position to buffer it.

Batteries. Real-time push, exactly like polling, can be expensive on a handset. Batch messages so the radio is woken less often, and do not add application-level keepalives you do not need — the connection is not dropped simply because the radio has gone idle.

Concept boundary — low latency here means one specific thing

It does not mean the packets travel faster. Switching from polling to a streaming transport does not change the round trip between client and server at all; that is propagation delay and it is set by physics and distance.
It means the queuing latency is gone. With polling, a message can be sitting ready on the server and still wait for the next client request — the delay is a function of your polling interval. With a persistent connection the server dispatches it the moment it exists.
Say it precisely: streaming eliminates message queuing latency, not propagation latency.

The full socket

WebSocket: both directions, text and binary — and every service the browser stops providing.

Tenth-grader version

  • This is the closest thing to a plain two-way pipe the browser will give you. Either side can send anything, any time.
  • It starts life as a normal web request that asks to be upgraded. Once the server agrees, HTTP is over and a different protocol takes the wire.
  • Messages are chopped into frames with a tiny header — a handful of bytes instead of the several hundred an ordinary request carries.
  • The catch is that nothing in the middle understands the pipe any more. No caching, no automatic compression, no labels on the data.
  • And the old problem is back: two messages cannot share the pipe at the same time, so one big message stalls the small urgent ones behind it.

The everyday version

A dedicated phone line between two offices, installed by asking the public exchange for it and then never hanging up. It is fast, private and either side can speak at any moment. What you lose the moment it stops being a normal call is everything the public network was quietly doing: no operator who can put a common enquiry through to a recorded answer, no line that compresses your speech, no record of what kind of call it was. And the line carries one conversation at a time — start reading a long document down it and the urgent one-word message waits until you finish.

WebSocket Bidirectional, message-oriented streaming of text and binary data. The closest API to a raw network socket in the browser. It is more than a socket: the browser adds connection negotiation, same-origin enforcement, interoperability with HTTP infrastructure, message framing, and subprotocol negotiation. vs a replacement for the others. It is not one. The design intent is to layer custom application protocols — and the price of custom is that the application must supply the state management, compression and caching the browser otherwise provides.

Framing overhead The bytes each frame costs you. Payload length is 0–125 inline; 126 means the next 2 bytes hold a 16-bit length; 127 means the next 8 bytes hold a 64-bit length. Server-sent frames cost 2–10 bytes. Client frames add a 4-byte masking key, so 6–14 bytes. Compare with 500–800 bytes plus cookies for an HTTP 1.x request, and about 5 bytes for an SSE message. What you gave up when HTTP stopped

  • Caching, by anyone. Stream an image over a socket and it will not be cached by the browser, by a CDN, or by any intermediary — so you may pay for the transfer repeatedly and drive far more traffic to origin. The same applies to video, text and everything else.
  • Compression. An ordinary request negotiates its transfer encoding; an SSE stream is all text so the whole session can be compressed. A WebSocket carries both text and binary, and compressing a session wholesale makes no sense because the binary parts may already be compressed — so compression has to be per-message, and until the extension is widely available, that means your code.
  • Metadata. There are no headers. If the receiver needs to know what a payload is, you must design that in: a fixed format agreed upfront, a consistent header inside the message, or a text message describing the binary message that follows.
  • Everything else the browser does with HTTP — authentication, state management, range requests. The handshake can use existing cookies to validate the user and decline the upgrade, and that is the one HTTP service that survives.
  • The strategy that follows: use the socket for what is genuinely non-cacheable — real-time updates and control messages — and let those messages trigger ordinary requests for anything an intermediary could usefully hold.

Infrastructure will fight you, and it is usually your own

HTTP is optimised for short bursty transfers, so servers, proxies and load balancers are routinely configured to time out idle connections aggressively — precisely the wrong behaviour for a long-lived session. There are three layers: your own routers, balancers and proxies; external proxies you do not control; and the client's own network, which may block this traffic entirely and is the reason to keep a fallback. TLS helps with the middle layer by tunnelling past intermediaries, and in practice significantly raises the success rate of establishing the session — though it does not stop anyone timing out an idle TCP connection. For the layer you own, the timeouts must be raised explicitly: a common proxy defaults to 60 seconds, and a common load balancer applies its connect, client and server timeouts only to the initial handshake, after which a separate tunnel timeout governs. Short timeouts are not stupidity — long idle sessions occupy memory and sockets everywhere — so before you add application keepalives, check the infrastructure.

Concept boundary — a socket is not an upgrade over the others

It is not faster at the packet level. Switching transports does not decrease the round trip; propagation latency is identical. What it eliminates is queuing latency, in both directions rather than one.
It is not a superset. An ordinary request inherits caching, compression, authentication and range requests from a browser that understands the protocol. A socket is opaque by design, and the flexibility is the loss of those services — the same fact, described from two sides.
So it is not a default. Pick it when you need frequent, latency-sensitive, bidirectional or binary traffic, and route everything else over HTTP.

Part 1 · Q1 — the Sydney dashboard and the uplink upgrade

(a) The Sydney figure is dominated by propagation delay, which is distance divided by signal speed and has nothing to do with anyone's link capacity. Upgrading the origin uplink reduces transmission delay — the time to push bits onto the wire — which is a function of the link's data rate and is independent of distance. Those are different delays with different causes, and the evidence in the question already rules out saturation: London, on the same origin and the same uplink, is fine. If the origin were saturated both would be slow. The remaining two delays, processing and queuing, also grow along the longer route because there are more hops, each reading headers and each with its own buffer.

(b) The floor: New York to Sydney is 15,993 km and gives 160 ms round trip along the great circle, and the real figure over existing cables is 200–300 ms; London to Sydney is a comparable order of magnitude. Take a round trip of roughly 250 ms. The arithmetic is distance ÷ ~200,000 km/s for the one-way time in fibre, doubled — noting that fibre is about 1.5× slower than light in a vacuum. The 78 requests are what turn that into 4.2 seconds: each round trip is spent serially unless the requests overlap, and with six connections per host and no multiplexing they overlap only six at a time. That is roughly thirteen sequential batches of 250 ms before you count server time — which lands in the right neighbourhood. Credit for observing that users are already past the 1,000 ms mark at which they mentally context-switch.

(c) Two structurally different fixes. Reduce the number of round trips — fewer resources, caching so repeat visits skip requests entirely, bundling, keepalive — which attacks how many times you pay the propagation delay, not the delay itself. And move the data closer with a CDN, which attacks the propagation delay directly and is the only lever on it. The thing that would genuinely lower the floor is a shorter cable along the great circle — and nobody does it here because laying cable is constrained by terrain, politics and cost, and is only justified where milliseconds are worth millions. Also creditable: better fibre and faster routers would help, but we are within about 1.5× of light speed so the ceiling on that strategy is roughly 30%.

Part 1 · Q2 — the gateway that never drops a packet

(a) Bufferbloat. The new gateway ships with large incoming buffers on the assumption that dropping packets should be avoided at all costs. When arrivals outpace service, packets are queued rather than dropped, and the time they spend in that buffer is queuing delay — which is high and, critically, variable. Throughput looks fine because bufferbloat is not a capacity problem: every byte still arrives, and a throughput test measures exactly that. It measures the wrong quantity for this fault.

(b) Because TCP's congestion avoidance uses packet loss as its signal. A dropped packet is how the network tells a sender to slow down. A buffer that absorbs the overflow suppresses that signal, so senders keep increasing their rate while the queue grows, and instead of a prompt backoff you get a long and unpredictable queue in front of every packet. The design goal is well-intentioned and the mechanism it breaks is the one that was keeping latency bounded — which is why the fix is not a bigger or a smaller buffer but a smarter one.

(c) The class of solution is active queue management — algorithms that deliberately manage queue delay rather than maximising buffer occupancy; the CoDel algorithm was proposed for exactly this and is available in modern kernels. Moving the video server closer would not fix it because a CDN reduces propagation delay, and the delay here is queuing delay being introduced by a device inside the office, after the packet has already arrived. Shortening the journey does nothing about a queue at the destination.

Part 2 · Q1 — the dashboard whose fourth chart never loads

(a) The browser opens at most six connections per host, and the two event streams and the long-lived polling request occupy three of them permanently. With the chart requests competing for what remains, the seventh outstanding request to that host simply hangs until one of the earlier ones completes — which is why closing a panel unblocks it. Idleness is irrelevant because HTTP 1.x has no multiplexing: a connection carries one exchange at a time whether or not any bytes are moving, so a stream that has sent nothing for ten minutes is still holding a full TCP stream.

(b) The underlying limitation is the absence of multiplexing, which is why browsers open several connections at all — and the per-host cap exists as a safety check, so that a client cannot saturate all available resources on a server, intentionally or otherwise. The irony is that the same check enables the reverse: exceed it on the client and all further requests to that host are blocked. Applications built on real-time delivery — sockets, event streams, hanging requests — are exactly the ones that hit it, so you can create a self-imposed denial of service against your own application.

(c) Two structurally different fixes. Consolidate the streams: carry all the update types over one connection, distinguishing them by event type, so you spend one slot instead of three — costing you a shared failure domain and some multiplexing logic in the application. Or move the streams to a different hostname, so they draw against a separate connection pool — costing an extra DNS lookup, a fresh TCP slow-start, and possibly a fresh TLS handshake, all of which hit high-latency clients hardest. Also creditable: reduce the number of long-lived connections generally, and do not add the fifth.

Part 2 · Q2 — the bundle that helped desktop and hurt everything else

(a) Three distinct trades. Mobile got worse because a 900 KB bundle must arrive in full before any of it executes — neither CSS nor JavaScript allows incremental execution — so on a high-latency, low-bandwidth link the time to first render is dominated by a transfer that used to be able to start executing in pieces. Repeat visits got worse because all 40 modules now share one cache key: a single change to any one of them invalidates and re-downloads the whole 900 KB. That is the loss of cache granularity, and it is the classic cost of bundling. The app is being killed because a sprite is decoded and held in memory as a bitmap at width × height × 4 bytes, regardless of the compressed file size and regardless of how little of it is on screen — the browser does not clip the unused part out of memory.

(b) No, and it may make things worse. Sharding multiplies the per-host connection limit; it does not reduce bytes, does not restore cache granularity, and does not reduce memory. It cannot even parallelise this download, because one 900 KB resource is a single request and lives on a single connection. What it does add is a DNS lookup and a fresh TCP slow-start per shard, which penalises exactly the high-latency mobile clients that are already the problem.

(c) Split the bundle: separate the stable core — frameworks and libraries that rarely change — from volatile application code, so a routine change invalidates only a small bundle; target roughly 30–50 KB compressed per script bundle, large enough to amortise per-request overhead and small enough to allow incremental execution; deliver first-paint-critical CSS separately from the rest. Stop spriting the icons at that scale, and inline only the small, page-specific ones — roughly under 1–2 KB, remembering base64 costs 33% and an inlined asset cannot be cached separately. What remains true regardless of protocol: eliminate unnecessary latency and minimise transferred bytes. Bundling and sharding are workarounds for a protocol that cannot interleave responses; caching, compression and deleting resources are not.

Section 9 continued

Part 3 · Q1 — the price stream with a gap after every reconnect

(a) On reconnect the browser does two things automatically: it reestablishes the connection after the retry interval, and if the server has been assigning IDs it sends the last one it saw back as a Last-Event-ID header. That is the whole of the browser's contribution. Replaying the missed messages is entirely the server's job — it must maintain a local cache of recent events and, on seeing that header, retransmit everything after that ID. A gap after every reconnect means either the server is not sending IDs at all, so the client has nothing to report, or it is sending them and ignoring the header on reconnect. Either way the browser is behaving exactly as designed.

(b) A reconnect cycle at a suspiciously round 60 seconds points at an idle or read timeout in the infrastructure — a proxy, load balancer or server tuned for short bursty HTTP rather than long-lived streams — or at an intermediary buffering the stream. The heartbeat is the wrong fix because it treats a configuration problem as a protocol problem: it does not raise anyone's timeout, it merely keeps traffic flowing so the timeout is not reached, which is a workaround that has to keep working forever. Its cost is paid by the mobile clients: a message every 5 seconds wakes the radio continuously, which is exactly the real-time push anti-pattern that drains handset batteries, and an SSE connection is not dropped merely because the radio has gone idle. The right sequence is: raise the timeouts you control, put the stream over TLS so intermediaries cannot buffer it, and batch real messages instead of manufacturing filler ones.

(c) Stop base64-encoding the charts into the stream. The stream is UTF-8 only by design, so binary costs a 33% byte expansion, and a streamed asset cannot be cached by the browser cache at all. Instead use the stream as a notification: push a small text message saying a new chart exists and where, and let the application fetch it with an ordinary request. The cost is one extra round trip per image. What it buys back: the asset becomes cacheable by the browser and by any intermediary, and it gets ordinary transfer encoding and compression — plus the 33% goes away. Also creditable: gzip the event stream itself, which is legitimate because it is an ordinary HTTP response.

Part 3 · Q2 — choosing a push transport for job progress

(a) The claim is wrong as stated. Changing transport does not decrease the round trip between client and server at all — propagation latency is a function of distance and the speed of light and is identical for polling, a stream or a socket. What both persistent transports eliminate is queuing latency: with 5-second polling, a status line can be ready on the server and still wait up to 5 seconds for the next request, whereas a persistent connection lets the server dispatch it the moment it exists. SSE and WebSocket are identical on this axis for server-to-client data; the socket adds nothing here. It only differs for messages travelling the other way.

(b) Three things SSE gives free that a socket does not. Automatic reconnection, at a default of 2–3 seconds and adjustable by the server with a retry field. Last-seen message tracking, where the browser remembers the ID and reports it on reconnect. And message parsing with no unbounded buffer — the browser finds the boundaries, extracts the payload and fires a DOM event per message, discarding what it has processed. Also creditable: session-wide gzip, which works because the stream is all text and an ordinary HTTP response; and the fact that it is ordinary HTTP, so it deploys through existing infrastructure with no new negotiation.

(c) The report should be delivered as a notification on the stream plus an ordinary HTTP request to fetch it — one extra round trip, in exchange for a cacheable, compressible, resumable download that any intermediary can serve, and no base64 tax. The WebSocket becomes right when the client needs to send frequently and with low latency — live cancellation, parameter changes during the run, a cursor or control channel — or when the downward data becomes genuinely binary and high-volume. Reaching for it merely because the client might send later trades caching, compression, reconnection and last-ID tracking for a capability nobody is using yet.

Section 9 continued

Part 4 · Q1 — the map that freezes when you pan

(a) Head-of-line blocking on the socket. Messages are delivered in exactly the order the client and server queue them, and although a message may be split into several frames, frames from different messages cannot be interleaved — there is no stream identifier in the framing layer. So a large tile message occupies the connection until it has been fully sent, and every cursor update queued behind it waits. A bigger send buffer makes it worse rather than better: it lets more large messages queue up ahead of the small ones, which is more of the same problem. The fix is at the application layer — split large messages, check what is already queued, and prioritise.

(b) The tiles stopped being cacheable. Delivered as ordinary HTTP resources they are held by the browser cache, by any intermediary proxy, and by a CDN, so a panned-over tile is fetched once and a popular tile is served to thousands of users from an edge node. Streamed inside a custom protocol they are opaque: nothing in the middle can identify or store them, so every client fetches every tile every time and all of that traffic terminates at the origin. Roughly ten times the forecast is exactly what losing a cache hierarchy looks like.

(c) Route by cacheability. Cursor positions, edits, presence and control messages go over the socket — genuinely non-cacheable, latency-sensitive, small, and needed in both directions. Tiles go over ordinary HTTP, fetched by the client, where they are cached by the browser and the CDN and compressed by ordinary negotiation; the socket can carry a message telling the client which tiles to fetch. The client-side check the application should be doing regardless is to monitor bufferedAmount before sending: send() is asynchronous and returns immediately, so a fast return is not evidence of delivery, and sending the next update only once the buffer has drained is what keeps a backlog from forming.

Part 4 · Q2 — a third of clients never connect

(a) The most likely cause is intermediaries that do not understand the protocol — explicit and transparent proxies, firewalls and carrier equipment between the client and the server. Because the connection begins as an HTTP Upgrade, such devices may blindly upgrade without understanding what they upgraded to, buffer frames, modify content, or misclassify the traffic as a compromised HTTP connection and drop it. Mobile clients are worst affected because their traffic crosses the most such equipment. The single change that addresses most of it is to use the secure scheme: negotiating TLS before the Upgrade handshake creates an end-to-end tunnel that intermediaries cannot inspect or alter, and in practice it significantly raises the success rate of establishing the session. Some networks block this traffic outright, which is why a fallback is still needed.

(b) Deaths at about a minute are idle timeouts in infrastructure the team operates. HTTP is optimised for short bursty transfers, so proxies, load balancers and servers are routinely configured to time out idle connections aggressively — one widely used proxy defaults to 60 seconds — and that is exactly wrong for a long-lived session. Raise the read and write timeouts explicitly on the proxy. The setting most often missed is on the load balancer: its connect, client and server timeouts apply only to the initial HTTP Upgrade handshake, and once the upgrade completes the connection is governed by a separate tunnel timeout that has to be raised on its own. Note also that TLS does not help here — it improves the odds of establishing the session but does not stop anyone timing out an idle TCP connection.

(c) The polyfill is a performance risk because the hard part of emulation is not the API but the transport: the fallbacks are polling, long-polling or a streaming request, and the library's choice decides what a fallen-back client actually experiences — polling reintroduces both the queuing latency and the full per-request HTTP overhead the socket existed to remove. Always prefer the native interface and hold the fallback to the same performance goals. The key-and-accept exchange in the handshake is a security policy against blind upgrades: it proves the server genuinely understands the requested protocol version rather than having switched protocols without knowing what it switched to. It does not authenticate anyone, does not encrypt anything, and does not protect against a transparent proxy that analyses and modifies data on the wire without announcing itself — which is precisely why the answer to (a) is a tunnel.

How to score yourself

Two marks, both quick. Completeness: did you name every element, and — for this competency especially — did you name the delay or the service at stake? “It'll be faster” is not an answer; “it removes queuing latency and leaves propagation unchanged” is. Phrasing: could you have said it in a design review without hand-waving? If you wrote “caching stops working” where the key says “the payload is opaque, so neither the browser cache nor any CDN can hold it and all the traffic lands on origin”, the label is there and the mechanism isn't — and the mechanism is what changes anyone's mind.
